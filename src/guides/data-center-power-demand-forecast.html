Every few months a new number for data center electricity demand makes headlines, and it rarely matches the last one. The forecasts are not measuring the same thing. Some count energy used over a year, some count peak load on one grid, and some count requests that may never be built.

This page puts the main public forecasts side by side, each with its source and date, then explains why they differ. For what data centers use now, see how much power a data center uses. Odd figures are rounded down and written as "over".

What do data center power demand forecasts say for 2030?

The main federal forecast is the Department of Energy's data center energy report, written by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. Its 2025 update, published in June 2026, estimated over 190 TWh of data center electricity use in 2024, or 4.7% of the US total. Its reference case reaches over 640 TWh in 2030.

Two other research bodies sit in the same range or below it. Berkeley Lab's own comparison describes the February 2026 update from the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) as spanning roughly 390 to 800 TWh for 2030, and reads the International Energy Agency's 2025 outlook as approximately 430 TWh for the United States. The IEA's worldwide Base Case is around 945 TWh in 2030.

Grid planners publish a different kind of number: peak demand in gigawatts. The table keeps the two kinds apart.

Why do data center forecasts disagree?

Different units. A TWh figure is energy used over a year. A GW figure is capacity at one moment. Berkeley Lab estimates that data centers use around 50% of the grid connection capacity they hold, so its reference case needs over 140 GW of interconnection capacity in 2030 to deliver over 640 TWh. A data center power consumption forecast in TWh will always look lower than a count of requested gigawatts.

Different assumptions. Berkeley Lab's own scenarios show how far one input moves the answer. If chip shipments run 15% lower, 2030 use falls to over 570 TWh. If AI inference takes more energy than expected, it rises to over 780 TWh. Stack the uncertainties together and the range runs from over 520 to over 840 TWh.

Different scope and dates. Berkeley Lab leaves out cryptocurrency mining and uses shipment data through the first three quarters of 2025. A study built on 2024 data is describing a different industry. The report also does not model whether the grid can deliver the power, so it is a forecast of demand, not of what gets connected.

How much is real and how much is speculative?

Requests to connect are not demand. In Texas, the grid operator reported over 470 GW of large loads seeking connection as of June 2026, about 90% of them data centers. The January 2026 reliability assessment from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) counts far less: Texas large loads totaling 45 GW by 2030, of which 23 GW are data centers.

The gap comes from how planners filter. NERC says data center projects are counted once they have advanced from speculative and exploratory stages into development commitments, and that new data center projections in Texas are reduced by almost 50% from the requested level to reflect how existing sites consume power. It adds that its forecasts are likely more conservative than predictions from the technology industry.

The Mid-Atlantic grid operator does something similar. Its January 2026 forecast requires firm commitments for near-term years and derates longer-term, non-firm projects. That forecast came out lower than the previous year's for the years through 2032. NERC notes that some large-load projects there and in Texas have slowed or failed to materialize in the near term, while requests for later years keep growing.

Lawmakers are filtering too. A 2025 Texas law requires a large load customer to disclose whether it is pursuing a substantially similar request elsewhere in the state. The mechanics are in our guide to the data center interconnection queue.

What do the forecasts mean for natural gas demand?

Natural gas supplied 40% of US electricity in 2025, according to the Energy Information Administration (EIA), so more electricity demand usually means more gas burned. In a March 2026 analysis, EIA tested faster data center growth. Its base forecast had gas generation rising 1.7% between 2025 and 2027. In the higher demand case the rise was 7.3%.

Longer term, EIA's 2026 outlook has the power sector using over 38 to over 50 billion cubic feet of gas a day in 2050 in most cases, up from over 35 in 2025. NERC counts 13 of 23 assessment areas adding gas-fired capacity over the next 10 years.

The agency sources on this page give no 2030 gas figure for data centers alone, so treat any single number for data center natural gas demand with care. Gas is not the only answer either. Hydro, nuclear, geothermal, and wind and solar with storage can all serve a data center, as our guide to how data centers are powered explains.

What do the forecasts mean for the grid and the queue?

Berkeley Lab attributes 33% of total US load growth between 2024 and 2030 to data centers. NERC expects summer peak demand across North America to rise by over 220 GW in 10 years, 69% more growth than it projected a year earlier, and says new data centers account for most of the increase. It finds 13 of 23 assessment areas facing resource adequacy challenges.

When demand grows faster than wires and power plants, the wait moves to the connection line. Berkeley Lab notes that some data centers may forgo grid interconnection altogether and build their own generation capacity. That is the case for behind-the-meter data centers: the schedule depends on the energy at the site, not on the grid's build-out.

How to read a new data center demand forecast

  1. Check the unit. TWh is energy over a year. GW is capacity. Don't compare one with the other.
  2. Check the geography. The world, the United States or one grid region.
  3. Check the date of the data, not the date of the press release.
  4. Check what is counted. All data centers or AI only, with or without cryptocurrency mining, requests or committed projects.
  5. Look for the range. A forecast with a single number is hiding its uncertainty.
  6. Compare it with estimated use today. Berkeley Lab estimates US data center electricity use rose 14% from 2023 to 2024.

The data center energy consumption forecast that matters for a site decision is the local one: what the utility or grid operator has committed to serve, and by when.

Frequently asked questions

How much electricity will US data centers use in 2030?

Berkeley Lab's June 2026 reference case is over 640 TWh, more than 11% of forecast US electricity use, with a range from 9.5% to over 15%. Its comparison puts EPRI's February 2026 range at roughly 390 to 800 TWh. The outcome depends on chip shipments, efficiency and how fast sites get power.

Why do data center demand forecasts differ so much?

They measure different things. Some report energy in TWh and others peak load in GW. Some cover the world, others one grid region. Some count every connection request and others only committed projects. Dates matter too: a forecast built on 2024 data will miss the AI hardware shipped since.

Are data center load forecasts overstated?

Raw connection requests are, and planners discount them. NERC reports that new data center projections in Texas are reduced by almost 50% from the requested level, and one regional forecast fell year over year for the near term. Estimated use is still rising: Berkeley Lab puts the increase from 2023 to 2024 at 14%.

How much natural gas will data centers need?

The agency sources on this page give no figure for data centers alone. EIA's March 2026 analysis found that faster data center growth would lift US gas-fired generation by 7.3% between 2025 and 2027, against 1.7% in its base forecast. How much of that becomes real depends on which sites get built and what energy they run on.

What is the difference between TWh and GW in a demand forecast?

GW measures capacity: how much power a site can draw at once. TWh measures energy: how much it uses over a year. A data center rarely runs at its full connection capacity. Berkeley Lab estimates average use at around 50% of interconnection capacity, which is why capacity forecasts look larger than energy forecasts.
