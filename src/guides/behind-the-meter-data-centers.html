Behind the Meter Data Centers: What It Means and the Trade-offs

A plain explainer: what the term means, how it compares with a grid connection and with temporary bridge power, and what you take on when you make your own electricity.

Updated October 28, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 6 min read

Quick answer

A behind the meter data center gets its electricity from generation on its own side of the utility meter, usually a power plant on the same site, instead of drawing it from the public grid. Developers choose it mainly to avoid long waits for a grid connection. In return, they take on fuel supply, backup and running a power plant.

"Behind the meter" started as a utility billing term. Now it comes up whenever people ask where new AI capacity will get its power. Large data centers need a lot of electricity, and many grids cannot connect them quickly.

This guide explains the term in plain words, compares behind-the-meter power with a normal grid connection and with temporary bridge power in one table, and lists the trade-offs that sales pitches tend to skip. It also covers when a grid connection is the better answer.

What does "behind the meter" mean?

Behind the meter means on the customer's side of the utility meter. The meter is the point where the utility measures the electricity it delivers. Anything on the utility's side is "in front of the meter". Anything on the customer's side, including generation the customer owns or contracts, is "behind the meter".

For a house, the classic example is rooftop solar with a battery. For a data center, it usually means a power plant built on or next to the site that feeds the building directly.

So the behind the meter meaning, in one line: you make the power where you use it.

Behind the meter describes where generation sits relative to the meter. It does not say what fuel is used. Gas engines, turbines, fuel cells, solar with batteries and, in time, nuclear can all be behind the meter.

Front of the meter vs behind the meter: what is the difference?

Front of the meter is the grid side: power plants, transmission lines and substations that sell or carry electricity to many customers. Behind the meter is the customer side: equipment that serves one site. The difference is who the power is for and who runs it.

  • Front of the meter. Large plants and grid batteries sell into the wholesale market or to a utility. Utilities, independent power producers and grid operators decide how they run. A data center connected this way is one customer among many.
  • Behind the meter. Generation serves one site. The owner or its energy partner decides how it runs. The site's power depends on that equipment and its fuel, not on the grid's spare capacity.

Many data centers sit in between. Some have a grid connection and add on-site generation. Others start with on-site power while they wait for the grid. The table below puts the three common setups side by side.

Why are data centers moving behind the meter?

The main reason is time to power. A large new load needs grid studies, upgrades and sometimes new transmission before a utility can serve it. In many regions that work takes years, and the line of projects waiting is long. You can read more in our guide to the data center interconnection queue.

Demand is the other half. A U.S. Department of Energy summary of a Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report found that data centers used about 4.4% of total U.S. electricity in 2023 and could use roughly 6.7 to 12% by 2028 (energy.gov, checked 2026-10-28). Grids have to add that load and connect new generation at the same time.

Behind-the-meter power sidesteps part of that wait. If the fuel and the generators are on site, the site does not need the grid to deliver its electricity. It still needs permits, equipment and a build. It is not instant. For how big these loads are, see how much power a data center uses.

Two other reasons:

  • Location. Sites can be built where energy already is, such as near gas pipelines, gathering systems or landfills, instead of where the grid has room.
  • Control. The site owner sees the whole power chain and can design it around the tenant's density.

What does behind-the-meter power for a data center look like?

Behind-the-meter power for data centers has four parts: fuel, generation, backup and a grid decision. Design each one for the load from the start.

  1. Fuel supply. For most sites today that is natural gas from a pipeline or a gathering system, or gas from a landfill. The supply has to be steady for the length of the lease, so it is usually contracted long term. Our guide to gas-powered data centers covers the fuel side in depth.
  2. Generation. Reciprocating engines or gas turbines are common. They are sized so that the site can lose a unit and still carry the load.
  3. Backup and spares. Extra generating units, a second fuel source where possible, and batteries or other storage to ride through switching. This is what turns on-site power into firm power.
  4. Grid decision. The site either runs as an island with no grid connection, or connects to the grid as backup or as a later main supply. Each path has different rules and costs.

Behind-the-meter solutions for data centers differ mostly in how they handle these four parts, so compare them part by part.

The trade-offs: what you take on behind the meter

Making your own power swaps one set of problems for another. Here are the main ones.

  • Fuel supply. The grid draws on many plants and fuels. A behind-the-meter site depends on its own fuel. Gas wells decline, pipelines need maintenance and contracts end. The fuel agreement should run at least as long as the lease, with a backup supply.
  • Backup. On the grid, a lost plant is someone else's problem. Behind the meter, it is yours. Redundant units, spare parts and storage cost money and take space, and they have to be designed in from the start.
  • Emissions. Gas generation emits carbon dioxide and local air pollutants, and it needs air permits. U.S. Energy Information Administration figures for 2023 put natural gas at 0.96 pounds of CO2 per kilowatthour of electricity, against 2.31 for coal (eia.gov, checked 2026-10-28). Lower than coal is not the same as low. Be wary of anyone who calls on-site gas clean.
  • Running a power plant. A data center operator that goes behind the meter is now also a generator operator: maintenance schedules, fuel logistics, emissions monitoring and trained staff on site. It is a different business, and it needs people who know it.
  • Rules. How on-site generation, co-located loads and grid backup are treated varies by market and is still changing in some places. Check with the local utility, grid operator and regulator early.
  • Stranded assets. If the grid arrives later and is cheaper, or the fuel runs out, on-site generation can end up underused. Lenders and investors ask about this.

None of these rule behind the meter out. They are why you should ask hard questions of anyone offering it.

When is a grid connection the better choice?

A grid connection is usually the better choice when one is available on your timeline. A utility connection spreads the work of fuel, backup and generation across a whole system. You pay a tariff and someone else runs the plants.

The grid also tends to win when:

  • You need to be close to a specific city or network hub where on-site generation is hard to permit.
  • Your buyers or your board require low-carbon power that on-site gas cannot provide.
  • The load is small enough that the local grid can take it without long upgrades.
  • You do not want to depend on one fuel supply.

Grid plus bridge power is the middle path: on-site generation, often rented, runs the site until the grid connection is ready. It can get a site running sooner, but it is temporary by design. Ask what happens if the grid date slips, and who pays for the bridge equipment while you wait. For a view of the whole build, see how long it takes to build a data center.

How to judge a behind-the-meter offer

If someone offers you behind-the-meter capacity, these questions separate a plan from a pitch.

  1. Where does the fuel come from, and how long is it contracted? Is there a second supply?
  2. How many generating units are there, and can the site carry the full load with one down? With two?
  3. What storage or switching covers the seconds while a unit trips and another picks up?
  4. Does the site connect to the grid at all? If so, for what, and under which rules?
  5. Which permits, including air permits, are in hand, and which are still pending?
  6. Who operates the plant day to day, and what experience do they have running generation?
  7. How is energy billed: bundled into the rent or passed through separately at cost?

Clear, specific answers are a good sign. Vague answers about "redundancy" or "clean energy" are not.

Frequently asked questions

What does behind the meter mean?

Behind the meter means on the customer's side of the utility meter. Electricity made there, for example by on-site generators or rooftop solar, is used by the site directly instead of being delivered and billed by the utility. For a data center, it usually means a power plant on the same site that feeds the building without going through the public grid.

Is behind-the-meter power cheaper than grid power?

Sometimes. It depends on fuel prices, the cost of building and running the generation and backup, and local tariffs. On-site power avoids some grid charges, but the site pays for its own plant and staff. The bigger value for many data centers is time: power when it is needed rather than years later. Compare full costs over the life of the lease, not just the fuel price.

Can a behind-the-meter data center also connect to the grid?

Yes. Some sites run as an island with no grid connection. Others connect to the grid for backup, to sell excess power, or as a later main supply once the connection is ready. Each option comes with its own studies, rules and costs, which vary by region, so make the decision early, with the local utility and grid operator.

What happens if on-site generation fails at a data center?

A well-designed site has more generating units than it needs, so it can lose one or more and keep carrying the load. Batteries or other storage cover the seconds while units switch, and a second fuel supply or grid backup covers longer problems. Without that design, a single failure can take the site down, so ask exactly how many units can be lost before the load is affected.

Is behind-the-meter power only natural gas?

No. Behind the meter describes where the generation sits, not the fuel. Natural gas is the most common choice for large data centers today because it can run around the clock. Landfill gas, fuel cells, solar or wind with batteries, and later nuclear are other options, each with different costs and steadiness.

Do you need the utility's permission for a behind-the-meter data center?

It depends on the setup and the region. A fully islanded site may need few utility approvals but still needs air, land-use and building permits. A site that connects to the grid, or sits next to an existing power plant, usually needs utility and grid-operator studies and approvals. Rules for large co-located loads are still changing in some markets, so check early.
