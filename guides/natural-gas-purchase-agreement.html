Gas Purchase Agreement: A Producer's Clause-by-Clause Guide

What you sign when a power user buys your gas at or near the lease: what each clause means, what to ask, and how the price at the wellhead is worked out.

Updated November 3, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 6 min read

Quick answer

A gas purchase agreement is the contract under which a producer sells natural gas to a buyer. It sets the term, how much gas and how firm, the price basis, quality, measurement, delivery point and remedies. When a power user buys gas at the lease, the clauses that matter most are firmness, take-or-pay, price and backup supply.

Most gas purchase agreement pages online are scanned contracts or notes written for lawyers. This one is for the producer across the table: each clause in plain terms, and the questions worth asking before you sign.

The focus is one kind of deal: a long-term sale to a buyer that burns the gas for power at or near the lease, such as a gas-powered data center. That buyer changes some of the usual math. It also asks more of you, mostly on firmness and term.

What is a gas purchase agreement?

A gas purchase agreement is a contract that commits a seller to deliver natural gas and a buyer to take and pay for it, on stated terms. Sellers often call the same document a sales agreement. A gas supply agreement is the same idea, common when the buyer is an end user such as a power plant.

Short-term trading deals often use a standard base contract, published by the North American Energy Standards Board, plus a short confirmation for each sale. Long-term deals with a single end user are usually written from scratch, because the term, volume and risks are particular to the site.

A gas supply agreement for power generation is that long-term kind. The buyer runs engines on your gas, so it cares less about trading flexibility and more about the gas arriving every day, for years, at a known price.

This is general information, not legal advice. Gas contracts vary by state, by lease and by deal. Have your own counsel review any agreement before you sign.

Firm vs interruptible gas: what is the difference?

Firm gas must be delivered and taken except when force majeure prevents it. Interruptible gas can be stopped by either side at any time, without liability. Those are the definitions in the standard base contract published by the North American Energy Standards Board, and they are a good plain test for any agreement.

Pipelines use the same split for transport. FERC describes firm service as the highest-quality service, with no planned interruption, paid through a reservation charge plus a usage charge. Interruptible transport costs less, with only the usage charge, but it is cut when higher-priority customers need the space.

For a power user the difference is practical. FERC notes that gas-fired generators struggled to buy gas during the winter storms of 2021 and 2022. A data center running AI hardware around the clock treats a gas interruption as an outage, so it signs for firm supply only.

Firm on paper also has to be firm in the field. If your gas drops whenever the oil side is shut in, it is interruptible in practice. That gas suits a load that can switch off; see what stranded gas is and which uses fit it and ways to monetize flare gas.

How does a take-or-pay gas contract work?

In a take-or-pay gas contract, the buyer pays for a minimum quantity of gas whether or not it takes delivery. It protects the seller's revenue. Deliver-or-pay is the mirror image: if the seller fails to deliver the firm quantity, it pays the buyer's cost of replacing it.

The details that decide how much these clauses are worth:

  • The minimum quantity. A daily, monthly or yearly floor, and how it is counted.
  • Make-up rights. Whether a buyer that paid for gas it did not take can take it later at no extra cost.
  • The remedy. The standard base contract offers two for a missed firm delivery: the cost of replacement gas, or the difference against a published spot price.
  • What excuses performance. Force majeure, planned maintenance and agreed curtailment days.

Take-or-pay has a history. EIA's account of FERC Order 436 (1985) describes pipelines with long-term obligations to pay producers for gas whether they received it or not, as their customers left. Courts sent the order back in 1987 for not dealing with those costs. A minimum-take clause is only as good as the buyer's ability to pay when its business changes.

How is the wellhead gas price worked out?

The wellhead gas price is what your gas is worth where it leaves the ground. EIA defines it as the value at the mouth of the well, generally the price a third party would pay in an arm's-length sale. In practice you see a hub price, and the wellhead price is what is left after the costs of getting the gas there.

That calculation is called netback. Start from the price where the gas is sold, then work backward:

  1. Benchmark price. Henry Hub in Louisiana is the US benchmark, per EIA.
  2. Basis. The local hub trades above or below the benchmark. In 2024, EIA reports, the Waha Hub in West Texas traded below zero on 42% of trading days because production outpaced pipeline takeaway.
  3. Transportation. Pipeline charges to the sales point, plus pipeline fuel burned in compressors.
  4. Processing. Fees, shrink and plant fuel, offset by any liquids value you are paid.
  5. Gathering and compression, plus lease fuel used in the field.
  6. What is left is your netback, before royalties and severance tax.

EIA's data shows how these layers stack. Its old wellhead price included gathering, compression and state severance charges, and hub spot prices include processing, gathering and transportation fees to the hubs. EIA stopped estimating a national wellhead price as of January 2013 because of data quality concerns.

Contracts state the price per MMBtu (one million British thermal units, or one dekatherm). So the heating value of your gas, set by testing samples, matters as much as the volume.

Where does an on-site buyer change the math?

When the buyer burns the gas at or near the lease, several netback deductions shrink or disappear. There is no long-haul transport and no pipeline fuel. Processing may come down to conditioning the gas for engines. And the price no longer depends on local basis or downstream pipeline space, so a takeaway squeeze like Waha's in 2024 does not reach you.

The trade runs the other way on four points:

  • Term. The buyer builds a plant around your gas, so it wants a long, firm commitment. You give up switching buyers when a pipeline arrives.
  • One buyer. Your revenue rests on one counterparty and its credit.
  • Firm delivery. You take on a deliver-or-pay duty and usually need backup supply to meet it.
  • Price shape. A fixed or floored price protects you when hub prices fall. A fixed price also gives up the upside.

The producers who gain most lose a large share of the hub price to basis and fees, or get shut in when takeaway is full. If you want to sell natural gas to a data center, see how gas-powered data centers work and why the buyer wants power behind the meter.

What should a producer do before signing?

Get your own numbers straight before you negotiate anyone else's.

  1. Work out your current netback from last year's statements: hub price, basis, every fee and the volume sold.
  2. Write down your production profile: daily rate, history, forecast and reserve life.
  3. Get a fresh gas analysis: heating value, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, water and liquids.
  4. Map your backup: other wells, a pipeline tie or a second gathering system.
  5. Check leases and existing dedications. Some acreage is already committed to a gatherer or purchaser.
  6. Have counsel review the draft, with the table on this page as a checklist.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a gas purchase agreement and a gas supply agreement?

Mostly the point of view. Both are contracts for the sale and delivery of natural gas. Buyers tend to say purchase agreement, sellers say sales agreement, and gas supply agreement is common when the buyer is an end user such as a power plant. What matters is the content: term, firmness, minimum quantities, price basis, quality, measurement and remedies.

What does take or pay mean in a gas contract?

Take-or-pay means the buyer pays for a minimum quantity of gas even if it does not take it. It protects the seller's cash flow and is common in long-term deals. Check how the minimum is counted, whether make-up rights let the buyer take paid-for gas later, and what excuses the buyer. The seller-side mirror is deliver-or-pay.

How is the wellhead gas price calculated?

Usually by netback. Start with the price at the sales point, often an index at a regional hub that trades at a basis to Henry Hub. Subtract transportation and pipeline fuel back to the field, processing fees and shrink, and gathering and compression. What remains is the wellhead value, before royalties and severance tax. EIA defines the wellhead price as the value at the mouth of the well, and stopped publishing a national estimate in 2013.

Can you sell natural gas directly to a data center?

Yes, if the data center generates its own power on site and your gas is firm. The buyer sets up engines or turbines near the gas and signs a long-term supply agreement, usually for years and with backup supply behind it. Flare, declining or interruptible gas does not suit it, because the hardware runs around the clock. Fiber within reach and land for the plant also matter.
