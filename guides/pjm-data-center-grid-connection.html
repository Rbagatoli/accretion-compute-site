PJM Data Center Grid Connection: Routes, Rules and What's Open

For plant owners, landowners and developers in the PJM states: the ways a data center can connect, what FERC changed in 2025 and 2026, and what is still being decided.

Updated November 15, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 5 min read

Quick answer

Data centers get a grid connection in PJM through the local utility as network load, or by co-locating at a power plant and taking one of three transmission services FERC ordered in December 2025. From June 1, 2027, new large loads that do not bring enough capacity may get interim service that is cut first in an emergency.

PJM runs the grid and wholesale markets for 13 states and the District of Columbia, including Virginia, which EIA says has the largest concentration of data centers in the world. It is also where the rules for data center connections are changing fastest. FERC found PJM's tariff unclear on co-located load in December 2025, set new terms in June 2026, and PJM is still filing the details.

This guide maps the routes in, says which are firm and which are not, and lists what is still open as of November 15, 2026. Queues in general are covered in the data center interconnection queue.

How does a data center connect to the grid in PJM?

The default route is through the local utility. The data center becomes a retail customer, the utility asks PJM for network service to serve the new load, and the transmission owner plans and builds any upgrades. The load is then counted in PJM's forecast and in the capacity the region must buy for future years. Texas runs a separate state process, covered in our guide to ERCOT data center grid connection.

Large loads are arriving faster than that process was built for. PJM's 2026 load forecast expects summer peak demand to reach over 222,000 MW by 2036, with 14 zones adjusted for data center growth. Two other routes have drawn attention as a result:

  • Co-location. The data center sits at or next to an existing plant and takes power from it, with or without drawing on the grid.
  • Behind-the-meter generation. The load is served by generation on the customer's side of the meter, netted against what it draws from the grid.

FERC has now set rules for both, and that is where most of the change is.

What FERC's co-location order changed

On December 18, 2025, FERC found PJM's tariff unjust and unreasonable because it did not say with enough clarity or consistency which rates, terms and conditions apply when generators serve co-located load. It directed PJM to write specific terms for co-location arrangements, to require one of three transmission services for co-located load, and to revise the rules for behind-the-meter generation.

PJM filed its compliance in February 2026. On June 18, 2026, FERC accepted it in part and rejected it in part, set rates, terms and conditions for the new services after a paper hearing, and gave PJM and the transmission owners 60 days to file further tariff changes.

The same day, FERC issued show-cause orders to six grid operators on speeding up large-load connections. PJM asked for a 90-day abeyance in that separate docket and said it expected to file in the first half of November 2026. Those filings, and FERC's orders on them, will settle much of what is still open.

The three transmission services for co-located load

Under FERC's orders, a customer taking transmission service for co-located load in PJM must pick one of three services:

  1. Network service (NITS). Full network service, like any other load. Where upgrades are needed first, the customer can take Interim NITS, a non-firm service available until all the network upgrades are built.
  2. Firm Contract Demand. A new firm service charged on a contract demand basis: a set amount of grid power rather than the site's full load, with the plant covering the rest.
  3. Non-Firm Contract Demand. A new non-firm service, also on a contract demand basis, which PJM can curtail under procedures set in the June 2026 order.

The choice comes down to how much firm grid service the site wants to pay for. A data center on Interim NITS or Non-Firm Contract Demand needs its own plant, or a way to shed load, for the hours when the grid curtails it. For an AI campus, which needs firm power around the clock, the plant on site has to carry the load in those hours. Behind-the-meter generation in PJM now

PJM's behind-the-meter generation rules let a customer net the output of generation on its side of the meter against the load it draws, which cuts the transmission and capacity charges it pays. FERC found those netting rules no longer just and reasonable and directed PJM to revise them, with a transition process for existing participants.

For a new data center, that means generation behind the meter while staying tied to the PJM grid no longer brings the same automatic savings. The details sit in the compliance filings still being worked out, so anyone planning a project should read the current tariff, not a 2025 summary.

A campus with no physical tie to the grid at all is a different case. It takes no transmission service and pays no PJM charges, though its generation still needs state air permits, and federal rules may apply too, as the EPA off-grid power guide explains.

Why the capacity shortfall matters for new data centers

PJM buys capacity three years ahead to make sure it can meet peak demand. The last three auctions all cleared at the price cap. The 2027/2028 auction fell over 6,600 MW short of the reliability target, and PJM said nearly 5,100 MW of an increase of about 5,250 MW in forecast peak load came from data centers. The 2028/2029 auction fell over 6,800 MW short.

PJM's board responded in July 2026 with an Interim Resource Adequacy Service. It applies to new large loads that do not bring enough capacity as of June 1, 2027. In an emergency, load reductions under this service come before other emergency steps. In plain terms, a new data center that connects without its own capacity may be the first to lose power when supply is tight.

What a plant owner or landowner should ask

  1. Is the plant's output already committed to the grid through capacity obligations or contracts, and when do those end?
  2. Would the data center take network, firm contract demand or non-firm contract demand service, and what happens on the days it is curtailed?
  3. Does the arrangement depend on behind-the-meter netting that FERC has ordered PJM to revise?
  4. Will the load bring its own capacity, or fall under interim service from June 1, 2027?
  5. Has the buyer read PJM's November 2026 filings, and how does it plan if FERC changes them?
  6. If there is no grid tie at all, which state air permits does the generation need?

Frequently asked questions

What is co-located load in PJM?

Co-located load is a large customer, such as a data center, that sits at or next to a power plant and takes power from it. FERC's December 2025 order requires a customer serving co-located load in PJM to take one of three transmission services: network service, including an interim non-firm version, Firm Contract Demand, or Non-Firm Contract Demand. The plant and the load still need a co-location arrangement under PJM's tariff.

What happened to behind-the-meter generation rules in PJM?

FERC found PJM's behind-the-meter generation rules no longer just and reasonable. Those rules let a customer net on-site generation against its load and so pay less for transmission and capacity. FERC directed PJM to revise the retail netting rules and to set a transition process for existing participants. The revised terms are part of compliance filings PJM was still making in late 2026.

What is PJM's Interim Resource Adequacy Service?

It is a service PJM's board approved in July 2026, first discussed as connect and manage. It applies to new large loads that, as of June 1, 2027, do not bring enough capacity to cover themselves. In a grid emergency, PJM cuts load under this service before it takes other emergency steps. A data center that brings its own capacity avoids being in that group.

When will PJM's co-location rules be final?

Not yet. FERC set rates, terms and conditions for the three new services on June 18, 2026 and gave PJM and the transmission owners 60 days to file tariff changes. In a separate docket on large loads, PJM said it expected to file in the first half of November 2026. FERC will then rule on those filings, so the tariff language may change again.

Does an off-grid data center need PJM approval?

A campus with no physical connection to the grid takes no transmission service from PJM, so the co-location services, netting rules and interim capacity service do not apply to it. It still needs state and local permits, including air permits for any generation that burns fuel, and EPA's July 2026 memo addresses how one federal program treats islanded generation.
