Brownfield Data Centers: What an Old Industrial Site Brings

For owners of idle plants, retired power plants and industrial yards: which parts of your site a data center can use, which it can't, and how to judge the fit.

Updated November 3, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 6 min read

Quick answer

A brownfield data center is built on land that had a past industrial use, such as a retired power plant, factory or rail yard. What helps is infrastructure that is slow to get new: gas service, a substation and grid tie, permits, industrial zoning, water, roads and fiber. Old buildings usually don't help, and contamination must be handled first.

If you own an idle plant, a retired power station or an industrial yard, you have probably read that data center developers want sites like yours. Some of that interest is real. But a developer is not buying your past. It is buying the few things on your site that would take years to get anywhere else.

This guide sorts those things out: which existing assets help, which have to come out, the cleanup and liability questions any former industrial site raises, and a self-check you can run before you contact anyone. If your land has never been built on, our guide to selling or leasing land to a data center fits better.

What is a brownfield data center?

A brownfield data center is one built on land that was used before, usually for industry, instead of on open land. The term has two meanings, and it helps to know which one a buyer means.

  • The building meaning. In the data center trade, brownfield can mean converting or upgrading an existing building, such as a warehouse or an older data hall.
  • The land meaning. In environmental law, a brownfield is a property whose reuse "may be complicated by the presence or potential presence of a hazardous substance" (CERCLA, quoted in EPA's January 2026 guidance).

This guide uses the land meaning: any data center on a former industrial site, whether or not contamination has been found. That includes retired coal and gas plants, closed mills and factories, and rail and truck yards. At a brownfield, cleanup standards are set and enforced by your state or tribe; EPA funds and supports the work but does not regulate it.

Why are old power plants and industrial sites drawing data center interest?

Because they often already have what takes longest to build: a path to large amounts of energy, industrial zoning and a permitting history. Time to power is the main constraint on new data centers, so a site that shortens it gets attention.

In January 2026, EPA published guidance on redeveloping Superfund and brownfield sites as AI data centers, written under Executive Order 14318 on speeding up data center permitting. It calls these sites strong potential candidates because of "preexisting infrastructure, likely industrial zoning and proximity to energy lines," while noting that local conditions and rules decide each case.

For an old power plant data center, the main draw is the grid connection. A September 2024 Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) paper notes that a retired coal plant leaves behind a point of interconnection: the equipment and the rights to use it. Reuse may still need a study and upgrades, but PNNL expects it to be faster and cheaper than a new connection, which can mean the long waits described in our guide to the data center interconnection queue.

A 2022 Department of Energy study of 157 retired and 237 operating coal plant sites, as hosts for new nuclear plants, estimated that reusing transmission lines, switchyards, cooling systems and roads could cut construction costs by 15% to 35%. Different project, same lesson: the value is in the infrastructure.

Timing is less certain than headlines suggest. EIA reported in April 2026 that 2.6 GW of coal capacity retired in 2025, against 8.5 GW planned. Retirement dates move.

What existing infrastructure actually helps a data center?

The assets that help are the ones that are slow or hard to get from scratch. If you want to repurpose an industrial site for a data center, list these first.

  • Gas service or a gas tap. For a data center that makes its own power behind the meter, a pipeline lateral, meter station or tap near the fence is often the most useful asset on the site, if the supply is firm. Our guide to gas-powered data centers explains why firm matters.
  • A substation and transmission tie. Most useful to developers who will draw power from the grid. Ask who holds the interconnection rights and whether they lapse after retirement.
  • Permits and a permitting record. Permits written for old equipment rarely carry over as they are, but a clean compliance record still helps.
  • Industrial zoning. It avoids a rezoning fight. Check that a data center is a permitted use.
  • Water rights, intakes and discharge permits. EPA's guidance even notes that groundwater treated in a cleanup could be reused for cooling.
  • Roads and rail. EPA lists road, rail or river access for heavy freight and fuel as a siting factor.
  • Fiber nearby. PNNL notes fiber often runs along the same transport corridors as old industrial sites.

Flat, graded ground also helps, if it sits outside ash ponds, capped areas and floodplain. For the full list of siting criteria, see our data center site selection guide.

What doesn't transfer: old buildings, equipment and ash

Most of what you can see on an old industrial site will not be reused. Data centers need high floor loads, dense power and tight control of heat and air, which old plant buildings rarely offer.

  • Old buildings. Column spacing, roof strength and floor loads usually rule them out. Developers that use factory-built modules need a pad, not a building.
  • Plant equipment. Boilers, turbines, stacks, coal handling and tanks generally come out, and older materials such as asbestos need careful handling. Ask early who pays for removal and who keeps the salvage.
  • Ash ponds and ash landfills. EPA describes coal ash as containing contaminants such as mercury, cadmium and arsenic. These areas carry their own closure duties; build around them, not on them.
  • Capped or controlled areas. EPA warns that construction can disturb capped soils or groundwater. Any cap or land use restriction limits where you can dig.

The useful parts of most sites are underground or on paper. The steel above ground is mostly a cost.

Cleanup, liability and permit risks to know about

This is general information, not legal or environmental advice. Talk to an environmental lawyer and a qualified consultant about your site.

Any buyer or tenant will look hard at contamination, because federal law can make an owner liable for cleanup. Your site's history will shape the price, the timeline and the contract.

  • Due diligence. Under EPA's All Appropriate Inquiries rule, a buyer seeking federal liability protection must complete the inquiry within one year before acquiring the property, some parts within 180 days. An ASTM E1527-21 Phase I satisfies it; a Phase II with sampling often follows.
  • Buying with known contamination. A bona fide prospective purchaser can buy property with known contamination if it bought after January 11, 2002 and meets ongoing obligations. That is why serious buyers want your past reports early.
  • State sign-off. EPA's guidance notes a deal may need a "No Further Action" letter from the state or tribal regulator, often through a voluntary cleanup program.
  • Remedy compatibility. The data center must fit around any caps, monitoring wells and treatment systems.
  • New permits and neighbors. New power equipment, stormwater, noise and building permits apply. EPA lists the community concerns that come up: water and energy use, noise, traffic and air quality.

Leasing instead of selling does not erase the site's history. You stay the owner of record, so how a lease divides cleanup and indemnity duties matters as much as the rent.

EPA's Redevelopment Mapper lists brownfields of over 100 acres with no reported redevelopment, and its regions run a free Prospective Purchaser Inquiry service for Superfund sites.

How can you tell if your industrial site is a fit?

Start with energy, then usable land, then paperwork. If the energy answer is weak, the rest rarely saves the site.

  1. Energy. Note what reaches the fence: a gas line or tap with its size and pressure, a nearby producer or gathering system, or a substation and its voltage. Is gas service firm or interruptible?
  2. Usable acres. Take out ash ponds, capped areas, remedy equipment, wetlands and floodplain, then measure the flat ground left. EPA says a 100 MW campus typically needs at least 100 acres; smaller loads fit on less.
  3. Paper. Gather Phase I and II reports, any No Further Action letter, deed restrictions, permits, utility and gas agreements, and easements.
  4. Access and zoning. Check distance to fiber, road and rail limits, the zoning, and how the town feels about new industrial use.
  5. What you want. Sell, lease or partner? Our guide on how to sell or lease land to a data center compares the three.

A site with these answers ready gets a faster reply if you want to sell industrial property to a data center developer. Many sites won't fit, usually because of energy, usable acres or unresolved cleanup.

Frequently asked questions

Can you build a data center on a brownfield site?

Yes, if the data center fits the site's condition and any cleanup in place. EPA's January 2026 guidance calls brownfield and Superfund sites strong potential candidates because of their infrastructure, zoning and nearby energy lines. It also says contamination must be addressed as needed for safe reuse, and construction must not disturb caps or groundwater.

Can an old power plant be turned into a data center?

The site can; the plant itself usually can't. A data center reuses the grid connection, the gas or water supply, the permits, the zoning and the roads. Boilers, turbines and stacks generally come out, and ash ponds stay off-limits. New buildings or factory-built modules go on the cleared ground.

Can I sell industrial property to a data center if it has contamination?

Often, yes. Federal law lets a buyer who does proper due diligence and meets ongoing obligations buy property with known contamination and still be protected. The contamination will still affect the price, the timeline and how the contract splits cleanup costs. Have your environmental reports ready and get legal advice first.

Does a retired power plant keep its grid connection?

The equipment usually stays, but the rights to use it depend on the grid operator's rules and on who holds them, often the plant owner or the utility. PNNL notes that reusing an existing point of interconnection may still need a study and upgrades, though it should be faster and cheaper than a new one. Ask early: rights can lapse after retirement.

What is the difference between a brownfield and a greenfield data center?

A greenfield data center goes on land that has not been built on. A brownfield data center goes on previously used land, often a former industrial site, or into an existing building. Brownfields can bring energy, zoning and permits. They also bring demolition, contamination questions and layouts that may not suit a data center.

Does my site need to be cleaned up before a data center developer will look at it?

No, but you should know where it stands. Developers need a clear picture: past Phase I and II reports, any No Further Action letter, the remedy in place and which areas are restricted. Known, managed contamination is often easier to plan around than a site with no records.
