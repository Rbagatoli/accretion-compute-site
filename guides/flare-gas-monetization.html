Flare Gas Monetization: A Producer's Decision Table

Eight things you can do with gas headed for the flare, compared on volume, gas life, steadiness, your capital, contract length and risk. Most flare gas suits a buyer that can switch off.

Updated November 3, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 6 min read

Quick answer

Flare gas monetization means selling or using gas you would otherwise burn. The main routes are a gathering tie-in, NGL recovery, CNG trucking, field power, interruptible compute such as Bitcoin mining, a firm on-site load such as an AI data center, and reinjection. The right one depends on volume, how long the gas lasts and how steady it is.

Flare gas monetization starts with an honest look at the gas itself. A new oil well can send a strong stream of associated gas to the flare for a few months, then fade. A pad behind a full gathering system can flare a steady volume for years. Both get called flare gas, and they need very different buyers.

This guide puts every realistic route side by side from the producer's chair: how much gas each needs, how long and how steadily it must flow, what you pay up front, how long you are tied in, and who holds the risk. For background, see what stranded gas is and why it gets stuck.

What does flare gas monetization mean?

Flare gas monetization means turning gas that would be burned at the site into revenue, or into a cost you no longer pay, such as diesel or bought power.

The scale is large. The World Bank's 2026 Global Gas Flaring Tracker puts global flaring at 167 billion cubic meters in 2025, gas worth an estimated US$54 billion. In the US, EIA data show about 335 billion cubic feet was vented and flared in 2024.

For one producer the question is narrower: which buyer can live with this gas? Six things decide it:

  • Volume. How much gas per day, from one well or a whole pad.
  • Gas life. How long the rate stays useful before decline.
  • Steadiness. Whether the flow holds day to day or stops when the oil side is shut in.
  • Producer capital. What you pay up front, if anything.
  • Contract length. How long you are tied to the buyer.
  • Risk. Who loses money if the gas falls off or a pipeline arrives.

What are the alternatives to flaring gas?

There are eight practical gas flaring alternatives for an onshore producer:

  • Gathering or pipeline tie-in. Sell it as ordinary sales gas. Best when volume and reserve life pay for the line and the system downstream has room.
  • NGL recovery. A skid or plant strips the heavier liquids for sale by truck. The leaner residue gas still needs a home.
  • CNG trucking. Compress the gas into tube trailers and haul it to a pipeline or customer. Trucking cost limits distance.
  • Field power for your own operations. Run generators, compressors and pumps on the gas instead of diesel or bought power. The load is small next to what a busy pad makes.
  • Interruptible on-site compute (Bitcoin mining). Generators and mining machines at the well. Mining can stop in minutes when gas dips and move when wells decline.
  • Firm on-site load (AI data center). A generation plant feeds AI hardware leased for years. It takes a large volume, but only if supply is steady, contracted and backed up. See how a gas-powered data center works.
  • Reinjection. Put the gas back in the reservoir, sometimes to support oil output. It earns nothing from the gas itself.
  • Shut in or curtail. Hold back the oil well until the gas has somewhere to go.

Who buys flare gas, and how is it priced?

Flare gas buyers fall into four groups: midstream companies that gather and process gas, service firms that compress or strip it on site, interruptible loads such as Bitcoin mining, and firm loads such as data centers. The producer can also be its own buyer, using the gas for field power.

Each buyer prices the gas differently. There is no published flare gas price and deals are private, so treat these as common shapes, not rates:

  • Index minus fees. Gathering deals usually pay a hub price less gathering, compression and processing charges. When the hub is weak, the netback can fall to zero or below.
  • Fee for service. CNG and NGL providers often charge per unit handled or share the liquids revenue.
  • Flat or floored price on site. On-site power buyers often pay a set price at the wellhead, or one with a floor.
  • Avoided cost. For field power, the value is the diesel or bought power you stop buying.

Capital follows the same split. Pipeline laterals and reinjection often need producer money. CNG, NGL skids and on-site power buyers usually bring their own equipment, and in return they want terms that protect it.

Flare gas to power: which kind of load fits?

Flare gas to power works with any of three loads, and the gas decides which. The flare gas power generation equipment is much the same in each case. What changes is how well the load copes when the gas stops.

  1. Your own field load. Small and forgiving. It uses part of the gas and can fall back to other fuel.
  2. An interruptible load. Bitcoin mining is the main example. It can take a large volume, shut off when pressure drops and leave when the wells decline. This is the natural fit for most flare gas.
  3. A firm load. An AI data center runs near full load around the clock for years. A fuel interruption is an outage, so it needs gas that is steady, contracted and backed up.

Here is the honest part. Most gas that reaches a flare is short-lived, on and off, or both: early associated gas from new oil wells, gas during upsets, gas from pads waiting for a line. That gas suits field power, CNG or interruptible compute. An AI data center fits only where the gas will hold steady for years or a gathering line can cover a shortfall. That is the behind-the-meter model at a gas site.

When is flare gas actually steady? Natural gas takeaway constraints

Flare gas is sometimes steady gas with nowhere to go. Natural gas takeaway constraints are the usual reason: production grows faster than the pipelines out of a basin, and gas that would sell in a normal market gets flared, curtailed or sold for less than nothing.

The Permian Basin shows how this plays out. EIA reported in February 2025 that gas at the Waha Hub in West Texas traded below zero on 42% of trading days in 2024, because production outpaced pipeline takeaway. In its May 2026 Short-Term Energy Outlook, EIA said most Permian gas is associated gas, that the region faced severe pipeline constraints, and that Waha spot prices had hit record lows, averaging below zero in eight of the previous nine months.

Relief is planned. EIA wrote in May 2026 that 44.9 Bcf/d of pipeline capacity additions are planned for 2026 and 2027, with 29.7 Bcf/d, more than two-thirds, in Texas, including projects meant to add Permian takeaway and clear the Waha bottleneck. EIA expected the constraints to ease later in 2026.

North Dakota tracks the causes. For June 2026 production, its Department of Mineral Resources reported 162.5 million cubic feet per day flared and statewide gas capture of 95.5%, against a 91% goal in place since November 2020. The state's Pipeline Authority splits flaring into wells with no sales at all, a lack of pipelines, and wells already selling some gas, which points to limits on existing infrastructure.

That second kind is the firm-buyer case. Gas from a connected, long-lived pad that flares or curtails because the system is full can be steady for years, and the gathering line can serve as its backup path. The catch is that new pipelines change the math, so a long contract has to plan for the day takeaway opens up. Our guide to gas purchase agreements walks through those clauses.

How do you pick a route for your gas?

Pick the route by matching the gas you have to the buyer that can live with it, not by the highest headline price. A short sequence helps:

  1. Separate the causes. Is the gas flared because there is no line, because the line is full, or because of upsets and well tests?
  2. Forecast the decline. Months of useful rate suggest mobile options. Years open the door to long contracts.
  3. Measure steadiness. Count how often and how long the gas stopped over the past year, and why.
  4. Check the backup. A gathering connection or other wells on the pad decide whether the gas can ever be called firm.
  5. Decide what you will put in. If you want no capital at risk, favor buyers that bring their own equipment.
  6. Match the term to the gas. No long firm commitment on short-lived gas, and no month-to-month deal on gas that will flow for years.

Flaring limits, capture targets such as North Dakota's and air permits for on-site generation can also change which routes are open.

Frequently asked questions

Who buys flare gas?

Midstream companies buy it when a gathering line can reach the well. Service firms handle it on site through CNG trucking or NGL recovery. Interruptible loads, mainly Bitcoin mining, buy it at the wellhead. Firm loads such as AI data centers buy it only when the supply is steady for years and backed up. The producer can also burn it for its own field power.

What is the price of flare gas?

There is no public flare gas price. Deals are private and the price depends on the buyer. Gathering deals usually pay a hub price minus fees, which can be near zero when the hub is weak. On-site power buyers often pay a set or floored price at the wellhead. Compare netbacks after all fees, not headline prices.

Does a producer have to invest money to monetize flare gas?

Not always. Pipeline laterals, compression and reinjection usually need producer capital. CNG providers, NGL service firms and on-site power buyers usually bring their own equipment, so the producer supplies the gas and site access. In return, those buyers want terms that protect their capital, such as a supply commitment.

What happens to a flare gas power deal when a new pipeline arrives?

It depends on the contract. Interruptible buyers usually have short terms and can move, so the producer can switch to pipeline sales. A long-term firm buyer will expect the gas to keep flowing, so the agreement should say how much gas is committed and what the pipeline is for. Often the new line becomes the backup supply that makes the on-site deal firm.

Which gas flaring alternatives actually earn money?

Gathering tie-ins, NGL recovery, CNG trucking and on-site power sales earn revenue from the gas. Field power earns by cutting fuel costs. Reinjection and shutting in avoid the flare without earning from the gas, though reinjection can support oil output.
