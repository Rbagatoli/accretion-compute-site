How Long Does It Take to Build a Data Center? Power Sets the Date

The building is the quick part. Getting enough electricity to the site is the slow part, and it is the step most timelines leave vague.

Updated October 28, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 6 min read

Quick answer

How long it takes to build a data center depends mostly on power, not on the building. Site work, permits, design and construction can overlap and move fairly quickly. Getting enough electricity to the site, through a grid connection, new substation equipment or on-site generation, is usually the slowest step, and it sets the opening date.

If you ask how long to build a data center, most answers give you a construction range and stop there. That misses the part that decides the date. A shell, cooling plant and halls are a known kind of project. The power behind them often is not.

This guide walks through each phase, from picking a site to commissioning, with what drives its length and what can shorten it. Then it explains why power usually sets the date, what "time to power" means, and the options that cut it down. Every duration on this page comes from a cited public source. Where there is no good public number, we say so and describe the step instead.

How long does it take to build a data center, phase by phase?

A data center goes through six broad phases: site selection, permits, design, construction, power delivery and commissioning. They don't run strictly one after another. Design starts before permits are issued. Construction starts before every piece of equipment arrives. Power work can start on day one. The total time is set by the longest chain of steps that have to happen in order, and for most projects that chain runs through power.

There is no reliable public dataset for how long each phase takes across the industry. Figures you see online vary widely and are rarely sourced. So the table below describes what drives each phase and what speeds it up, rather than quoting a single number for each.

Skip "how long is construction?" Ask which step is on the critical path, and when it finishes. For most new capacity today, that step is power.

Why does power, not construction, usually set the date?

Power usually sets the date because it depends on equipment and processes the builder does not control, and many of them are backed up nationally. A building can be designed in parallel, built by several crews at once and fitted out in stages. A grid connection waits on a utility study, on upgrades to lines and substations, and on heavy electrical equipment with long order books.

Three things tend to sit on the critical path:

  • The utility's study and upgrade work. A large new load has to be studied by the utility. If the local lines or substation can't carry it, upgrades are needed first, and those are scheduled by the utility, not by you.
  • Large transformers. The U.S. Department of Energy reported in 2024 that lead times for large power transformers have become exceptionally long, with 36-month lead times commonly quoted and some reaching as much as 60 months. Before COVID-19, an order could be placed with a lead time of less than a year.
  • New generation for the grid. Where the grid needs new power plants to serve new demand, those plants wait in their own queue. Berkeley Lab found that in 2025 the median time from a power plant's interconnection request to a signed agreement was well over 3 years, and the time to commercial operation exceeded 5 years in the regions with data.

That last figure is about power plants joining the grid, not data centers. No national dataset tracks how long a large load waits for service. It varies a lot by utility and location. Still, the two are linked. A region short of generation and transmission will struggle to serve a large new load quickly, whatever the builder does. We cover how those queues work in our guide to the data center interconnection queue.

The federal government has said as much. In its Speed to Power work, the Department of Energy summarised stakeholder input that large loads need connection on timelines shorter than current development allows, and named interconnection queues and permitting as barriers.

What is time to power for data centers?

Time to power is the time from deciding to build at a site until firm electricity is available there at the scale you need. It is different from build time. A finished hall with no power is an empty room. For an AI company planning capacity, time to power is the number to watch.

It helps to split it into parts:

  1. Securing a source. A signed agreement with the utility, or a fuel supply and generation plan for on-site power.
  2. Getting the equipment. Transformers, switchgear and, for on-site power, the generating units. Many of these are made to order.
  3. Building the power path. Substation work, lines, or the on-site power plant, plus the connection to the halls.
  4. Energising and testing. Bringing power up in steps and proving it under load before tenants rely on it.

When someone quotes you a date, ask which of those four parts are already done. A site with a signed utility agreement and equipment on order is in a different place from a site that has only applied.

How can you shorten the time to build a data center?

Shorten the power path. Faster construction helps less than you might expect, because construction is rarely the longest chain. There are three main options, and they can be combined.

1. Choose a site where power is already there. Former industrial sites, retired plants and land next to substations with spare capacity can skip some of the upgrade work. This is why "powered land" has become its own category. The catch: such sites are scarce, and spare capacity can be taken by someone else before you sign. Landowners weighing this can read our guide on selling or leasing land to a data center.

2. Generate power on site. A behind-the-meter data center makes its own electricity next to the halls instead of waiting for grid service. That takes the utility's queue off the critical path. It does not remove the power path. On-site power has its own steps: a firm fuel supply under contract, generating equipment that may itself have long lead times, air and other permits, and backup units so a single failure does not take down the load. For how gas fits into this, see gas-powered data centers.

3. Build in phases. Bring up a first block of capacity while later blocks are still being built or powered. A tenant can start with part of its deployment and grow on the same site. It only works if the site, power plan and lease are designed for it from the start.

A few smaller steps also help. Order long-lead electrical equipment as early as the design allows. Run permits and design in parallel. Fix the tenant's density and cooling needs early, because changing from air to liquid cooling halfway through redesigns the halls and the power distribution.

None of these makes power instant. They move the slowest step earlier, or replace it with one you control more directly.

What should you ask before trusting a data center timeline?

Ask where the power comes from and what is already signed. Any provider can quote a construction schedule. The questions below show whether the power behind it is real.

  • Is power coming from the grid, from on-site generation, or both?
  • If grid: is there a signed agreement with the utility, or only an application? Which upgrades does the utility still need to make?
  • If on-site: is the fuel supply contracted for the length of the lease? Is it firm, with backup supply, or can it be interrupted?
  • Are transformers, switchgear and generating units ordered, and what delivery dates do the orders show?
  • Which permits are issued and which are still pending?
  • Can the capacity come online in phases, and are the phase dates written into the lease?
  • What happens to your lease if the power date slips?

If a provider answers these plainly, including "not yet", you can plan around the timeline. A date with no answers behind it is a hope.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to build an AI data center?

The building itself is rarely the slowest part. AI data centers need far more power per rack than older facilities, so the power path, whether a grid connection or on-site generation, usually decides the date. Higher density also means more electrical equipment and heavier cooling, often liquid. Many of those parts are made to order, so the design has to be fixed early. There is no reliable public figure for a typical AI build. Ask any provider which parts of the power path are already signed and ordered.

Why do data centers take so long to get power?

A large new load needs utility studies, often grid upgrades, and heavy equipment such as large transformers. The U.S. Department of Energy reported in 2024 that 36-month lead times for large power transformers are commonly quoted. Where the grid also needs new generation, those power plants wait in their own queue, which Berkeley Lab found ran well over 3 years to a signed agreement in 2025. The builder controls none of these directly.

Is it faster to build a data center with on-site power?

It can be, because the utility's queue drops off the critical path. But it is not automatic. On-site generation needs a firm fuel supply under contract, generating equipment that may have its own lead times, air permits, and backup units. Whether it is faster depends on the site: how close the fuel is, whether the equipment is on order, and how long local permits take. Compare the full power path for each option, not just the grid wait.

What drives the cost to set up a data center?

Power and cooling usually drive most of the cost to set up a data center, more than the shell. The main items are the electrical path (substation, transformers, switchgear, backup, or an on-site power plant), the cooling plant sized for the rack density, the building and halls, fiber, and the land. In a colocation or leased model the tenant usually buys its own computing hardware separately. Prices vary too much by site and design to quote a useful range here.

Can a data center open in phases?

Yes. It is a practical way to shorten the wait for first capacity. A site can bring up a first block of halls and power while later blocks are still being built. It works best when the site, the power plan and the lease are designed for phases from the start, with each phase's delivery date written into the lease and commissioning done phase by phase.
