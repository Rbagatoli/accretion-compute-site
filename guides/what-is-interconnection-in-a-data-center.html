What Is Interconnection in a Data Center? Three Meanings

For AI teams, buyers and landowners who hear the word used three ways: what each kind of interconnection is, how it is bought, and how much an AI site needs.

Updated November 15, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 5 min read

Quick answer

In a data center, interconnection usually means the network links between customers, carriers and clouds, made with cross-connects in a meet-me room. It can also mean the fiber links between data centers, or the grid connection that brings power in. An AI training site needs diverse fiber routes and bandwidth more than a dense web of cross-connects.

Ask a colocation sales team what interconnection means and you will hear about cross-connects and ecosystems. Ask a utility and you will hear about studies, queues and substations. Both are right. The word covers several different things, and mixing them up leads to bad site decisions.

This guide takes the three meanings in turn: links inside a building, links between buildings, and the link to the power grid. It ends with what an AI site needs from each.

What does interconnection mean in a data center?

Data center interconnection has three common meanings:

  1. Network interconnection inside the building. Physical links between the networks of different customers, carriers and cloud providers that sit in the same facility.
  2. Data center interconnect (DCI). Fiber links between two or more data centers, so they can share data or act as one system.
  3. Grid interconnection. The connection between the site and the power grid, and the utility process that approves it.

The first meaning dominates in colocation, where a building's value rises with the number of networks inside it. The third dominates in power and real estate, where the wait for a grid connection now sets many project timelines. An AI campus built next to its own energy has to think about all three, but not equally.

What is a cross-connect?

A cross-connect is a point-to-point cable link between two customers in the same colocation data center. That is how one large colocation operator defines it, as quoted in a 2023 industry research note. In practice it is a fiber or copper cable run from one customer's cage to another's, usually through a meet-me room, so traffic between them never touches the public internet. Contracts and price lists spell it both ways, cross-connect and cross connect; data center operators mean the same thing.

Cross-connects are bought from the data center operator, usually as a recurring monthly charge for each cable. The same 2023 note said they are a core recurring revenue stream for colocation companies and make network-rich buildings more valuable as hubs where organizations connect to each other.

More links bring more ways to fail. The note cited a 2022 survey in which the two most common causes of connectivity-related outages were misconfiguration or change management failure, reported by 43% of respondents, and a failure at a third-party network provider, also 43%.

Meet-me rooms, carriers and carrier-neutral sites

A meet-me room (MMR) is a secured room where carriers bring their fiber into the building and where cross-connects between networks are made. Outside fiber enters the property through conduits, called outside-plant entrances, and runs to the MMR. From there, cabling runs to the customers' space.

A carrier-neutral data center lets any carrier bring fiber in and sell service to tenants, instead of tying them to one provider. Competition among carriers gives tenants more choice of routes and prices. A building with dozens of carriers and an internet exchange on site is often called a carrier hotel.

The Open Compute Project's hyperscale site assessment sets a high bar for these features. Its top rating calls for three diverse outside-plant fiber entrances, a carrier-neutral site with room to expand, paired isolated MMRs each with a dedicated fiber entrance, and two diverse pathways from two MMRs to the IT space. Those are useful questions to ask of any site, including one far from a city.

Links between data centers

Data center interconnect, or DCI, joins two or more facilities with fiber. Operators use it to copy data between sites for backup, to spread a workload across buildings, or to move a trained model from where it was trained to where it serves users.

DCI is bought in two main forms. Dark fiber is unlit fiber the customer leases and lights with its own equipment, which gives the most control and capacity. Wavelengths are a share of a carrier's lit fiber, sold at a set speed, which is simpler to buy but less flexible.

For an AI campus outside a metro area, data center fiber connectivity, through DCI and long-haul routes, matters more than cross-connects. The campus needs a reliable path to the networks, clouds and storage it works with, and that path needs a backup. EPA's 2026 guidance on redeveloping contaminated land as AI data centers lists redundant high-capacity fiber over diverse routes as a requirement. Data center site selection covers how fiber fits with power, land and water.

Grid interconnection: the other meaning

In the power world, interconnection means connecting a generator or a large load to the grid. The US grid in the lower 48 states is itself made of three main interconnections, Eastern, Western and Texas, which mostly run independently of each other. In Texas, ERCOT is the interconnection, the balancing authority and the grid operator at once. How a data center connects there is covered in our guide to ERCOT data center grid connection.

A data center that wants grid power applies to its utility, pays for studies, waits for upgrades and signs an interconnection agreement. For large loads that process can take years, and the queues are long. The data center interconnection queue guide explains why. In PJM, a data center can also co-locate at a power plant; see PJM data center grid connection.

A site that makes its own power behind the meter, with no grid tie, skips this kind of interconnection entirely. It still needs network interconnection, because GPUs that cannot reach the outside world are no use to anyone.

How much interconnection does an AI site need?

It depends on the work. Training traffic between GPUs stays inside the building, on the cluster's own high-speed network. The links to the outside world carry datasets in, checkpoints and finished models out, and management traffic. They need bandwidth and a second route more than they need to be a few milliseconds from a city.

Serving users is different. Inference for chat, search or trading benefits from sitting close to users and to internet exchanges, often within a mile, as EPA's guidance notes for latency-sensitive sites. Those workloads fit metro colocation better.

A practical checklist for an AI site outside a metro area:

  • At least two fiber routes that do not share a trench or bridge.
  • Enough capacity on each route to move training data and checkpoints on schedule.
  • Space for carriers to bring in more fiber later.
  • A clear plan for links to the clouds and storage the tenant already uses.

Frequently asked questions

What is a cross-connect in a data center?

A cross-connect is a point-to-point cable link between two customers in the same colocation data center, usually made in the meet-me room. It lets their networks exchange traffic directly, without crossing the public internet. The data center operator installs it and usually charges a recurring monthly fee for each one, which is why colocation companies count cross-connects as a core revenue stream.

What is a meet-me room?

A meet-me room is a secured room in a data center where carriers bring their fiber into the building and where cross-connects between networks are made. The Open Compute Project's hyperscale assessment calls for a pair of isolated meet-me rooms, each with its own fiber entrance, so one failure does not cut the building off. From the meet-me room, cabling runs through separate pathways to the space where customers keep their equipment.

What is the difference between a cross-connect and a grid interconnection?

A cross-connect is a network cable between two customers inside a data center, ordered from the data center operator. A grid interconnection is the connection between a site and the power grid, approved by the utility after studies and often years in the queue for large loads. They share a word and little else. A site with its own power behind the meter needs the first but not the second.

What does carrier-neutral mean?

A carrier-neutral data center lets any network carrier bring fiber into the building and sell service to tenants, instead of tying them to one provider. Tenants get more choice of routes and prices, and can buy from more than one carrier for backup. The Open Compute Project's hyperscale site assessment lists carrier neutrality, with room to expand, among its top-rated features.

What is data center interconnect (DCI)?

Data center interconnect is the fiber that links two or more data centers so they can share data, back each other up or run a workload across sites. It is usually bought as dark fiber, which the customer lights with its own equipment, or as wavelengths at a set speed from a carrier. For an AI site outside a city, DCI and long-haul fiber matter more than local cross-connects.
