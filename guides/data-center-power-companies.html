Data Center Power Companies: Who Sells Power to AI Campuses

The six kinds of company that put electricity into a data center, how each one gets paid, and who carries the risk when fuel prices move or a line runs late.

Updated November 15, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 5 min read

Quick answer

Data center power companies come in six kinds: the local utility, competitive suppliers and power marketers, independent power producers, on-site generation providers, energy owners, and developers who build next to the energy. Which one matters depends on whether a campus buys grid power, signs a contract with a plant, or makes its own power on site.

Search for data center power companies, or data center energy companies, and most results are lists of equipment makers: generators, switchgear, batteries. Those firms sell hardware. This guide is about the companies that deliver the electricity itself, how each one is paid, and who carries the risk when fuel prices move or a grid upgrade runs late.

It is written for energy owners and landowners weighing an offer, and for AI tenants who want to know where their power comes from. No company is named here; lists of names date quickly, and the roles do not. For the energy sources themselves, see how data centers are powered.

Who supplies power to a data center?

Electricity reaches a data center by one of three paths, and each path has its own kind of supplier.

  • Through the grid. The local utility delivers the power. In states that allow it, a power marketer may sell the energy while the utility still runs the wires, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration.
  • By contract with a plant. A power producer or a utility signs a power purchase agreement with the data center owner. The power usually still crosses the grid, unless the campus sits next to the plant.
  • On site. Generation is built at the campus, behind the meter, and the data center uses it directly. Berkeley Lab's 2025 report to the Department of Energy notes that some data centers may forgo a grid connection altogether.

Large campuses often mix these. A grid-fed site keeps on-site generators for backup, and an on-site plant may keep a grid tie for emergencies. The behind-the-meter guide explains where the meter sits in each case.

Utilities and competitive suppliers

The utility is the default power company for any data center. EIA sorts US utilities into investor-owned companies, municipal systems, member-owned cooperatives and federal power agencies. The utility owns the local wires, usually builds the substation that feeds the campus, and sets the terms of the connection under rules its state regulator approves.

Some utilities generate their own power. Many buy it from other utilities, power marketers, independent power producers or wholesale markets. Where retail choice exists, a data center can buy energy from a competitive supplier and pay the utility only for delivery.

What a utility cannot do quickly is add hundreds of megawatts of new load. Each large request needs studies and often new lines and substations, and new rules in several states put more of that cost on the large customer. How that lands on everyone else's bills is covered in the data center electricity cost impact guide.

Independent power producers and power purchase agreements

An independent power producer owns plants and sells their output under contract instead of to its own retail customers. A data center owner signs a power purchase agreement (PPA) for part of a plant's output: a wind or solar farm, a gas plant, a hydro dam or a nuclear station.

A PPA fixes the price of energy and can tie a buyer's use to a named source. It does not move electrons to the campus by itself. Unless the plant is next door, the power still crosses the grid, so the data center still needs a utility connection and still waits its turn for one.

That is why co-location, a data center built at the fence line of an existing plant, drew so much attention. FERC ruled on co-located load in the largest US grid region in December 2025, and on June 18, 2026 it ordered six grid operators to justify or reform their rules, including those for co-location and behind-the-meter generation. Behind-the-meter power companies

A behind-the-meter power company builds generation at the campus and supplies it straight to the data center, with no utility meter in between. The plant can be engines or turbines on natural gas or landfill gas, fuel cells, or wind and solar with storage. It can also be a direct line from a hydro, nuclear or geothermal plant next door.

Two business models are common:

  • Power as a service. A provider owns the engines or turbines and charges the data center a fee for energy or capacity. The data center avoids the capital cost and pays the provider's margin for the life of the contract.
  • Owner-operated. The data center developer owns and runs the plant itself. It carries the capital cost and the operating work, and in return it controls the plant and keeps the margin.

Berkeley Lab and Brattle's 2026 review of retail power prices lists on-site generation and bring-your-own power among the ways to add large loads with less strain on the grid. The trade-off is permitting. Anything that burns fuel needs an air permit, which state and local air agencies issue in most cases, according to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. For a plant with no grid tie, see EPA rules for off-grid data center power.

Energy owners: hydro, nuclear, gas, landfill, wind and solar

The last group rarely calls itself a power company. It owns the energy: a hydro or nuclear plant, a geothermal field, a gas producer with steady wells, a landfill with gas from its waste, or a wind or solar farm. For a data center, these owners hold the scarcest input of all, firm energy in a place where a campus can be built.

Their options run from selling fuel or power under contract to leasing land and letting a developer build beside them. What decides the fit is steadiness. An AI tenant needs power around the clock for years, so firm sources come first: hydro, nuclear, geothermal, steady gas or landfill gas, and wind or solar paired with enough storage. Gas that is interruptible or declining suits a load that can switch off better than an AI campus.

Offers to energy owners differ most on three points: who pays for the build, who runs the plant, and what happens if output drops. Read those clauses before the price.

How to choose a power partner

  1. Start with the energy. Is it firm, around the clock, for years? If not, an AI campus is the wrong buyer.
  2. Ask where the meter sits. Grid delivery brings queue and tariff rules. Behind-the-meter power brings air permits and the job of running a plant.
  3. Ask who owns the plant. A provider that owns it earns a margin for the whole term. A developer that owns it carries the capital and the operating work.
  4. Ask who pays for what. Network upgrades, substations, fuel and backup each need an owner in the contract. Find each one before signing.
  5. Ask what happens when things change. The contract should cover declining output, a tenant that grows, and a grid line that arrives later.

Frequently asked questions

Who supplies electricity to data centers?

Most data centers get electricity from their local utility, which delivers it over the grid. Where retail choice exists, the energy itself may come from a competitive supplier or power marketer while the utility runs the wires. Large campuses add contracts with power producers, and some build their own generation on site, behind the meter. Many use more than one of these at once, for example grid power with on-site backup.

Do data centers buy power directly from power plants?

Many sign power purchase agreements with plant owners, but the power usually still travels over the grid and the data center still needs a utility connection. Buying straight from a plant with no grid in between needs the campus to sit next to the plant, with a direct tie behind the meter. Grid rules for that kind of co-location are still being written: FERC ruled on it for the largest US grid region in December 2025 and asked six grid operators to justify their rules in June 2026.

What is a behind-the-meter power company?

It is a company that builds generation at or next to a data center and supplies it directly, without the power passing through a utility meter. The provider may own the plant and sell power as a service, or the data center developer may own and run the plant itself. The source can be engines or turbines on natural gas or landfill gas, fuel cells, wind and solar with storage, or a direct line from a hydro, nuclear or geothermal plant.

Which energy companies sell power to AI data centers?

Every kind on this page does: utilities, competitive suppliers, independent power producers, on-site generation providers, and owners of hydro, nuclear, geothermal, gas, landfill, wind and solar assets. What an AI buyer needs from any of them is firm power around the clock for many years. That favors sources that run steadily, such as hydro, nuclear, geothermal and steady gas, and it rules out interruptible supply unless storage or backup covers the gaps.

Do data center power companies need an air permit for on-site generation?

Yes, for anything that burns fuel. EPA says state and local air agencies issue most air permits for data centers, and engines and turbines must meet federal emission standards. In July 2026 EPA said its Acid Rain Program does not apply to an islanded plant with no grid connection, but that memo leaves the other permit duties in place. Plants that burn nothing, such as wind, solar, storage or a line from a hydro plant, have no air permit for the generation itself.
