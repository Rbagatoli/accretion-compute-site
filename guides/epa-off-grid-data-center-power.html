EPA and Data Center Power Off the Grid: What the 2026 Memo Means

For energy owners, landowners and developers planning power on site: what EPA said in July 2026, what it left open, and which rules apply to each kind of power. Not legal advice.

Updated November 15, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 6 min read

Quick answer

In July 2026, EPA said a power plant with no physical tie to the grid, built to serve a data center, falls outside the Acid Rain Program. That settles one program for data center power off the grid. The plant still needs an air permit, usually from the state, and its engines and turbines must meet federal standards.

In July 2026, EPA's Office of Air and Radiation answered a question several companies and states had asked: does the Acid Rain Program cover a power plant that is not connected to the grid and only serves a data center next door? EPA said no. News coverage made it sound as though off-grid power had been freed from air rules. It has not.

This guide reads the memo and EPA's own data center resource page closely. It sorts the rules by power source, including the sources that burn nothing, and lists every EPA action with its date. For how a behind-the-meter campus works in general, see behind-the-meter data centers.

What does EPA say about off-grid data center power?

The memo, dated July 16, 2026, deals with the Acid Rain Program, set up under Title IV of the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments to cut sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from the power sector. The program applies to fossil fuel units owned or operated by a utility, which EPA's rules define as any person that sells electricity, when they serve a generator that would have had to report on the 1990 edition of the Department of Energy's Form 860.

That 1990 form only covered generators selling electric energy primarily for use by the public. EPA reads that language to require a grid connection. A plant that makes power only for a private user and is not connected to the grid falls outside the form's reporting requirement, and therefore outside the Acid Rain Program.

The memo is short and careful about its limits. It says:

  • It is not a final determination or final agency action for any particular facility.
  • It does not cover every scenario, or other exemptions such as those for cogeneration plants and solid waste combustors.
  • It does not address any other Clean Air Act program.
  • Acid Rain permitting is generally run by state air agencies, whose rules can differ from the federal ones, so sources should ask their state.

What counts as islanded?

In EPA's words, an islanded facility has no physical connection to the utility grid. The example in the memo is a planned plant of about 500 MW of simple-cycle gas turbines and reciprocating engines built to power an adjacent data center owned by a third party, and nothing else.

A plant that is islanded today does not stay outside the program for good. If the owner later connects it to the grid, EPA says it may become subject to the Acid Rain Program, and advises owners to keep the program's permit rules in 40 CFR Part 72 in mind when they plan.

Islanded is a narrower word than behind the meter. A campus can sit behind the meter and still keep a grid tie for backup or export. Under the memo, only a site with no physical connection at all counts. Rules for grid-tied behind-the-meter generation are changing separately in PJM, as our guide to PJM data center grid connection explains.

Which rules still apply to engines and turbines?

Most of the federal rules that matter for on-site power do not depend on a grid tie. EPA's data center resource page lists the standards for the two most common power sources:

  • Stationary combustion turbines: new source performance standards (NSPS), amended January 15, 2025, and national emission standards for hazardous air pollutants (NESHAP).
  • Stationary engines: NSPS for compression ignition engines (40 CFR part 60 subpart IIII) and spark ignition engines (subpart JJJJ), and the NESHAP for reciprocating engines (40 CFR part 63 subpart ZZZZ).
  • Portable engines: emission standards for nonroad engines.

Backup generators have their own rules. EPA guidance from 1995, still listed on its page, treats 500 hours a year as the default for estimating an emergency generator's potential to emit, with other estimates allowed case by case. Certain emergency engines may run up to 50 hours a year in non-emergency situations. EPA says hours run under the Department of Energy's 2026 emergency orders do not count toward that limit.

Engines and turbines that burn landfill gas or other fuels fall under the same kinds of standards. AI data center environmental impact covers emissions and water in more depth.

Off-grid power that burns nothing

The memo is about fossil fuel units, and so are the engine and turbine standards. Off-grid data center solutions that burn no fuel sit outside both: wind and solar with storage, hydro and nuclear. These sources still need other permits, such as land use, water and, for nuclear, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission. They do not need a combustion air permit for the power plant itself.

A direct tie to an existing plant next door is a third path. If the plant already holds its air permits and only its buyer changes, the questions shift to the plant's permits, contracts and any grid commitments. If it stays connected to the grid, it is not islanded, and the Acid Rain Program applies as before.

A site whose main power burns nothing may still keep diesel or gas engines for backup. If it does, the emergency engine rules above apply to them. Gas-powered data centers covers fuel supply for sites that use gas.

Who issues the permit?

In most cases, the state. EPA's resource page says state and local air agencies issue most air permits for data centers, under programs EPA has approved as meeting federal minimums. EPA staff also consult with permit agencies and applicants case by case, through EPA's regional offices.

Which permit depends on how much the plant can emit. A plant that could emit above federal thresholds needs a major source permit under New Source Review and, often, a Title V operating permit. A plant whose permit limits keep it below those thresholds goes through a state minor source program instead. EPA guidance from 1989 and 1995, listed on the same page, covers how permit conditions can legally limit a source's potential to emit.

State timing can matter more than federal rules. In Texas, for example, the state environmental agency paused permits for infrastructure that directly supports data centers in 2026; the ERCOT grid connection guide has the dates.

EPA actions on data centers, 2025 and 2026

  • January 15, 2025: final amendments to the NSPS for stationary combustion and gas turbines.
  • May 1, 2025: a clarification EPA described as helping to power data centers.
  • September 9, 2025: a permitting reform announcement aimed at faster construction of power generation.
  • January 23, 2026: a roundtable with a data center trade group on Clean Air Act resources and reliability.
  • May 11, 2026: a proposed rule to redefine "begin actual construction" in New Source Review, published May 13, 2026. It is still a proposal.
  • July 16, 2026: the islanded generation memo.
  • July 2026: guidance on emission reduction credits for nonattainment New Source Review permits.

Rules in this area have changed several times in two years. Check EPA's resource page and the state agency before relying on any summary, including this one.

Questions to settle before you build

  1. Will the plant have any physical connection to the grid, now or later?
  2. Which state agency issues the air permit, and is it a major or minor source permit?
  3. Which NSPS and NESHAP apply to the engines or turbines you plan to use?
  4. How many hours will backup engines run, and how is their potential to emit counted?
  5. Is the area in nonattainment for any pollutant, which changes the permit and may require offsets?
  6. Has a lawyer read the memo against your site, since EPA made no final call for any facility?

Frequently asked questions

Does the Acid Rain Program apply to an off-grid data center?

According to EPA's July 16, 2026 memo, not if the power plant has no physical connection to the grid. EPA reads the program's definitions to require a grid connection. The memo is guidance, not a final determination for any facility, and it does not cover other Clean Air Act programs, so a plant can be outside the Acid Rain Program and still need other air permits.

Does an off-grid data center need an air permit?

Usually yes, if its power burns fuel. Gas engines, turbines and diesel backup generators need an air permit, most often from the state or local air agency, and must meet federal emission standards for engines and turbines. Whether it is a major or minor source permit depends on how much the plant could emit. Power that burns nothing, like wind and solar with storage, needs no combustion air permit.

What happens if an islanded data center later connects to the grid?

EPA says that a plant built without a grid connection may become subject to the Acid Rain Program if the owner connects it to the grid later. The memo advises owners to keep the program's permit requirements in 40 CFR Part 72 in mind when they plan. It does not say exactly how coverage would work in that case, so any later tie needs its own review.

How many hours can data center emergency generators run?

EPA treats runs under the Department of Energy's 2026 emergency orders as emergency operation, with no hour limit while the emergency lasts, and those hours do not count toward the 50 hours a year that certain emergency engines may run in non-emergency situations. For permits, EPA guidance uses 500 hours a year as the default for estimating an emergency generator's potential to emit, unless the agency allows another figure.

Is EPA's islanded power memo a final rule?

No. It is a memo from EPA's Office of Air and Radiation to its regional offices, clarifying how EPA reads existing Acid Rain Program rules. EPA says it is not final agency action and makes no final determination for any facility. State air agencies generally run Acid Rain permitting and their rules can differ, so the state's reading matters as much as EPA's.
