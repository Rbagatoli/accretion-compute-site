Data Center vs Nuclear Power Plant: Scale, Water and Timing

One uses power and the other makes it. Here are the two next to each other, with public figures, and the ways an AI campus can actually run on nuclear energy.

Updated November 15, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 5 min read

Quick answer

Compare a data center vs nuclear power plant and the gap in scale is wide. A hyperscale AI campus draws 100 MW or more; an average US reactor makes about 1,000 MW and ran at a 91% capacity factor in 2025. One reactor could feed several campuses, through a grid contract, a site beside the plant, or a restart.

People search this comparison for two reasons. Some want to picture the scale: is a data center as big as a power plant, and which one uses more water? Others own a plant, or land beside one, and want to know whether nuclear powered data centers make sense next door.

This guide answers both with dated public figures from the U.S. Energy Information Administration, the Department of Energy, the International Energy Agency and the National Renewable Energy Laboratory. No plant, owner or buyer is named. For every other energy source, see how data centers are powered.

How does a data center compare with a nuclear power plant?

A data center consumes electricity. A nuclear plant produces it. The useful comparison is how many of one the other can serve, and what each needs from the land and water around it.

On the plant side, EIA counts 94 commercial reactors at 54 plants in 28 states as of March 2026, with over 98,000 MW of net summer capacity. Single reactors range from about 520 MW to about 1,400 MW. The fleet is old, at about 44 years on average, and EIA credits uprates, upgrades that add capacity to existing reactors, with keeping its output high even as the number of reactors fell.

On the data center side, the International Energy Agency puts a hyperscale AI-focused campus at 100 MW or more. Berkeley Lab's 2025 report to the Department of Energy found that US data centers used 4.7% of the country's electricity in 2024. The two industries are now big enough to matter to each other.

How many data centers can one reactor run?

Divide the fleet's capacity by its reactor count and the average US reactor is about 1,000 MW (our arithmetic from EIA's figures). On paper that covers ten AI campuses of 100 MW each. Three things shrink that number in practice:

  • The output is already sold. A running reactor already feeds the grid. Power for a new campus has to come from its current buyers, from an uprate, or from a restart.
  • Running hours. EIA reports a 91% average capacity factor for nuclear plants in 2025, higher than any other kind of plant. That is excellent, but an AI campus needs power in the other hours too, so it needs a second source or the grid behind it.
  • Grid rules. Moving a reactor's output from the grid to a campus beside it raises questions about who pays for the transmission system both still lean on. Regulators are settling those questions now.

A plant with two reactors can often support more than one large campus. The binding limit is usually contracts and grid rules, not physics.

Which uses more water: the data center or the plant?

On data center vs nuclear power plant water consumption, the plant usually uses more. A National Renewable Energy Laboratory review of power plant water use puts a nuclear plant with cooling towers at a median of 672 gallons consumed per MWh. A gas combined-cycle plant with cooling towers uses 198 gallons per MWh, a dry-cooled one about 2, and wind uses none.

Run that through a 100 MW campus that uses full power every hour of the year, 876,000 MWh (our arithmetic), and the water used to make its electricity comes to over 580 million gallons a year on nuclear with cooling towers, over 170 million on a tower-cooled combined-cycle plant, and about 1.7 million on a dry-cooled one.

Not every nuclear plant has cooling towers. EIA notes that some draw water from lakes, rivers or the ocean instead. The NREL review finds those once-through systems withdraw far more water than towers but consume less of it. The data center's own cooling is a separate figure, and air-cooled designs use no water on site. The data center water usage guide has both sides in full.

Three ways data centers use nuclear power

  1. A contract over the grid. The data center signs a power purchase agreement with a plant owner and takes the power through its normal utility connection. It gets a nuclear-backed supply on paper, but it still needs a grid connection and still waits for one.
  2. Co-location at the plant. The campus is built next to the reactor and takes power across a short tie. In the largest US grid region, FERC's December 2025 order requires a co-located load to pick one of three transmission services. Its June 18, 2026 order set the rates and terms, and the PJM grid connection guide explains each option.
  3. New nuclear megawatts. Restarts of closed plants and uprates at running ones add capacity that is not yet promised to anyone. That makes them the easiest nuclear megawatts for a new buyer to contract.

Small modular reactors appear in most headlines about nuclear and AI. This guide sticks to plants that run today and to restarts and uprates with public dates, because those are the nuclear megawatts a campus can plan around now.

Restarts and uprates: where new nuclear supply comes from

The Department of Energy is backing three restarts of closed plants: about 800 MW in Michigan, the first US restart of a reactor that had entered decommissioning; 835 MW in Pennsylvania; and 615 MW in Iowa. For the Iowa plant, which stopped running in 2020, DOE closed a loan of up to $1.9 billion in September 2026, with the restart still pending Nuclear Regulatory Commission approvals.

Uprates add more. DOE's Project UPRISE fact sheet of October 27, 2026 says the program has identified about 4.7 GW of added nuclear capacity, 95% of its 5 GW goal by 2029, with conditional loan commitments of up to $5 billion and up to $4.2 billion announced in October 2026.

The pool of restart candidates is limited. EIA, citing the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, lists 19 reactors at 15 sites in some stage of decommissioning, and most of those will never run again.

What a plant owner should weigh

For the owner of a nuclear, hydro or other firm plant, a data center next door is a new kind of customer: one that wants power every hour for years and will pay to be close. Before talking terms, settle these points:

  • Land near the switchyard. A campus needs flat, buildable acres close to the plant, outside any area the plant's licensing and security rules keep clear.
  • Output that is free to sell. Check what existing contracts and grid obligations already claim, and whether an uprate could add capacity for the new buyer.
  • The grid operator's rules. In some regions a co-located load must take transmission service even if it rarely uses the grid. Know which service applies before pricing the deal.
  • Who builds and runs what. Decide who owns the tie line, the substation and any backup supply, and who pays if the plant is offline.

Frequently asked questions

Can one nuclear plant power a data center?

Yes. One average US reactor of about 1,000 MW could in principle supply several 100 MW AI campuses. In practice most of its output is already sold, the plant does not run every hour, and grid operators set rules for loads built beside a plant. The usual answer is a share of one plant's output, with a second source or the grid covering the hours when the reactor is offline.

Does a nuclear-powered data center use more water?

The power plant does, if it uses cooling towers. NREL's review puts a nuclear plant with cooling towers at a median of 672 gallons consumed per MWh, against 198 for a tower-cooled gas combined-cycle plant and about 2 for a dry-cooled one. For a 100 MW campus running all year, that is over 580 million gallons on nuclear. The data center's own cooling is separate, and air-cooled designs use no water on site.

Why are data centers buying nuclear power?

Because it runs almost all the time. EIA reports a 91% average capacity factor for US nuclear plants in 2025, higher than any other kind of plant, and nuclear reactors do not produce air pollution or carbon dioxide while operating. An AI campus needs power around the clock for years, and many buyers also want power that emits no carbon dioxide. The catch is that the output of running plants is already sold to someone.

Are closed nuclear plants being restarted?

Three are, with Department of Energy financing: about 800 MW in Michigan, 835 MW in Pennsylvania and 615 MW in Iowa. The Michigan plant is the first US reactor to restart after entering decommissioning. The Iowa plant closed in 2020 and its restart still needs Nuclear Regulatory Commission approvals. EIA lists 19 reactors at 15 sites in decommissioning, so few other candidates remain.

What is co-location at a nuclear plant?

Co-location means building a data center next to the plant and feeding it across a short tie instead of through the wider grid. Grid operators treat it in different ways. In the largest US grid region, FERC ordered in December 2025 that a co-located load take one of three transmission services, from full network service to a new non-firm option, and set the rates and terms in June 2026.
