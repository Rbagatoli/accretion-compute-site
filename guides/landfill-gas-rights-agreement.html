Landfill Gas Rights Agreement: What Owners Should Check

For county, city and private landfill owners: who owns the gas, how royalties and fixed fees compare, who keeps the credits, and the clauses to read before you sign.

Updated November 14, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 6 min read

Quick answer

A landfill gas rights agreement is a contract in which a landfill owner gives a developer the right to take the site's gas and use it for energy. In return the owner is paid a royalty, a fixed fee or a share of revenue. The terms that matter most are price, credits, collection duties, term and exit.

If a developer has offered to buy your landfill's gas, or your current gas contract is ending, the document in front of you may be called a gas rights agreement, a gas lease or a gas purchase agreement. The names differ. The questions an owner should ask are the same.

This guide covers the contract, not the technology. For how the gas is collected and how much a site can produce, see landfill gas to energy. The points below come from the U.S. EPA's project development handbook and from a gas lease on the public record.

What is a landfill gas rights agreement?

A landfill gas rights agreement gives one party, usually a project developer, the right to the gas a landfill produces for a set number of years. The developer builds and runs an energy project on or next to the site, and the landfill owner is paid for the gas.

The U.S. EPA sorts the contracts around a project into three groups: agreements to sell power, agreements to buy the gas, and agreements covering the environmental attributes. A gas rights deal can touch all three, and it needs a right to use the ground the plant sits on, in a site lease or inside the gas agreement.

Who owns landfill gas?

EPA's handbook treats the landfill owner as the party that grants the rights to the gas and is paid for them. Where one party owns the landfill and another operates it, check the operating contract first. It may already say who holds the gas.

What a new agreement has to spell out is how far the transfer goes:

  • Exclusivity. Does the developer get all of the gas, or only what its plant can use? In one gas lease on the public record, the developer received all rights, title and interest in all of the landfill's gas.
  • Footprint. Does the grant cover future cells? In the same lease, the developer's duties ran across the whole landfill, expansions included.
  • Metering. EPA notes that gas sales are usually set on the energy delivered, so the meter point decides what you are paid for.

How do landfill gas royalties work?

EPA describes the usual pattern this way. When a third-party developer is involved, the developer typically pays the landfill owner a royalty for the rights to use the gas. In general the developer makes a minimum payment for access and shares a percentage of the upside when the energy sells into higher-value markets. The size of the royalty depends on how the two sides share project costs and risks.

Where the gas is sold outright, EPA lists three pricing methods. A fixed price is set for the energy delivered and usually escalates over time. An indexed price follows current natural gas prices, at a discount. A hybrid holds the price fixed for a period and then converts to an index. EPA calls a floor price essential to reducing the seller's risk.

A royalty tied to revenue raises its own questions. Revenue from what: power, gas, credits or all three? Before or after costs? In the public gas lease, the monthly royalty was tied to the developer's gas sales revenue, net of direct third-party costs such as transportation, and the landfill owner had the right to audit the payments. Ask for both: a written definition of revenue and the right to check the math.

Who keeps the environmental credits?

EPA's handbook says agreements have been relatively clear about gas rights, but not as clear about newer markets and incentives such as renewable energy certificates, tax credits and greenhouse gas credits. Its advice is that all parties make sure ownership of the environmental attributes is clearly defined.

  • Renewable energy certificates. Each one stands for the environmental attributes of one megawatt-hour of renewable power delivered to the grid.
  • Greenhouse gas credits for destroying methane. EPA notes that a project generally does not qualify if the landfill is already required by regulation to collect and control its gas.
  • Renewable fuel credits under the federal Renewable Fuel Standard, where the gas becomes vehicle fuel, plus credits under state clean fuel programs. EPA's handbook lists California, Oregon and Washington.

Two cautions. The same attribute can't be sold twice. And read the definitions: in the public gas lease, the environmental attributes and emission credits were written into the definition of the processed gas product the developer sold. If you want to keep your credits, the contract has to say so in words.

Who runs the collection system?

In one arrangement, the owner keeps the wells, pipes and flare and delivers gas that meets an agreed amount and quality. In the other, the developer takes over. In the public gas lease, the developer owned the collection system during the term, had exclusive control over its operation, maintenance and repair, with ownership passing to the landfill owner when the agreement expires or is terminated.

Either way, compliance stays with someone, and the agreement must say who. Federal rules require landfills with a design capacity of at least 2.5 million megagrams and 2.5 million cubic meters, and emissions of at least 34 megagrams of non-methane organic compounds per year, to install and operate a gas collection and control system.

EPA notes that the landfill owner is typically responsible for air permit requirements related to surface emissions, even with a developer on site. It also flags a conflict worth planning for: a developer may make decisions that favor energy revenue, while the owner's priorities are managing gas migration and emissions. Set who adjusts the wells, who pays for new ones, and who runs the backup flare when the plant is down.

Term, decline and exit clauses

Gas output is not flat. It rises while a landfill takes waste and falls after it closes, which the landfill gas to energy guide covers with EPA's figures. The clauses to read closely:

  • Term. EPA notes that power sales contracts for these projects vary greatly, from 1 to 15 years. The gas rights term should line up with the energy sale and the life of the equipment.
  • Quantity and quality guarantees. Contracts may include a minimum amount and quality of gas delivered, and a minimum the buyer must take or pay for. EPA warns that landfills that are closed, or closing soon, should be cautious about aggressive limits. For how take-or-pay works, see our guide to a natural gas purchase agreement.
  • The developer's way out. In the public gas lease, the developer could surrender the agreement on 180 days' notice if gas could not be recovered in commercial quantities, a test left to the developer's own judgment. Ask what you are owed if that clause is used.
  • End of term. Who owns the wells, pipes and plant when the agreement ends, and who removes what?

Who buys landfill gas?

EPA lists four reasons landfill gas is sold: as fuel for a nearby boiler, kiln or furnace; to power a generating plant; for cleanup to pipeline quality; and to a third-party developer that buys the rights and decides the use. Many landfill gas to energy companies sit in that last group. They turn landfill gas to electricity for the grid, upgrade it for a pipeline, or use the power on site for a load such as a data center.

To find landfill gas buyers, EPA describes a request for proposals as a competitive and fair basis for comparing developers, though it notes that private landfill owners don't normally issue one. Its handbook suggests comparing proposals on more than price: up-front payments, revenue sharing, who holds the credits, what the developer plans to spend on the wellfield, and how much of the gas quality and availability risk the developer takes on.

If you want to sell landfill gas on good terms, get more than one kind of offer.

Checklist before signing

  1. Confirm who holds the gas rights now: the owner, an operator, or a developer under an older contract.
  2. Get a current gas model and a measured flow, so both sides are pricing the same gas.
  3. Choose the structure: royalty, fixed price, indexed price or your own project.
  4. Define revenue, set the floor and keep the right to audit.
  5. Write down who owns each kind of credit.
  6. Assign the collection system, the compliance filings and the backup flare.
  7. Set the term, both sides' exits and who owns the equipment at the end.

This guide is general information, not legal, tax or investment advice. Have a lawyer who knows landfill gas contracts review the full set of documents.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a landfill gas rights agreement last?

There is no fixed length. EPA's handbook notes that power sales contracts for landfill gas projects vary greatly, from 1 to 15 years, and that lenders prefer a term at least as long as the financing. Expect a developer to ask for gas rights that cover its energy sale.

What is a typical landfill gas royalty?

No public standard exists. EPA describes the common structure as a minimum payment for access to the gas plus a percentage of the upside from energy sales, and says the value varies with how the developer and the landfill owner share expenses and risks. Compare offers on the definition of revenue, not on the percentage alone.

Can a landfill owner sell the gas and keep the environmental credits?

Yes, if the contract says so. EPA advises that all parties make sure ownership of the environmental attributes, including the rights to greenhouse gas reductions, is clearly defined. An owner can sell the gas and keep the credits. What can't happen is the same attribute being sold twice.

Does a gas rights agreement move compliance duties to the developer?

Only as far as the contract and the permits allow. EPA says project developers are responsible for making sure the energy project complies with applicable regulations, and that the landfill owner is typically responsible for air permit requirements related to surface emissions.

What happens to a landfill gas rights agreement when the landfill closes?

That depends on the term and exit clauses. Either way, the gas declines once waste stops arriving. EPA cautions landfills that are closed or closing soon against agreeing to aggressive gas quantity or quality limits. Federal rules also require gas monitoring for 30 years after closure.
