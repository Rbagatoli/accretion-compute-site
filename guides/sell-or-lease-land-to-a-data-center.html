How to Sell or Lease Land to a Data Center Developer

The news covers the record land deals. This is the part it skips: what a developer checks, what powered land means, how a deal runs, and how to tell if your site is a fit.

Updated October 28, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 6 min read

Quick answer

To sell land to a data center developer, your site usually needs power first: grid capacity or firm on-site energy such as pipeline gas. Then fiber in reach, flat buildable acres, water or a cooling plan, workable zoning and road access. You can sell outright, sign a long ground lease or partner. Most deals start with a short call.

Search for how to sell land to a data center and you mostly find news: a county sells a training campus, a family sells farmland near a substation, a price per acre makes headlines. They rarely say why the land sold.

This guide is for the owner. It covers what developers check and why power sets the value. Then it compares selling, leasing and partnering, walks a deal from first call to signed agreement, and lists the questions that protect you. Most land does not fit, and we say so.

What do data center developers look for in land?

Developers look for power first and land second. A flat, cheap parcel with no realistic path to large amounts of electricity rarely gets a second look. A small or awkward parcel with energy on site or next door can be worth a lot.

  • Power or energy on or near the site. Spare capacity at a nearby substation or transmission line, or a firm fuel source on the land itself, such as pipeline gas, a gas gathering system or landfill gas.
  • Fiber in reach. A long-haul or regional fiber route close enough to connect without a long, slow build.
  • Flat, buildable land. Firm ground, outside flood zones, with room for the building, the power plant or substation, and later expansion.
  • Water or a cooling plan. Some designs use a lot of water; others use closed-loop or air cooling. Either way, the developer will ask.
  • Zoning and permits. Industrial zoning, or a clear path to it, and an air permit pathway if power will be generated on site.
  • Road access. Heavy trucks need to reach the site during construction and for fuel, parts and equipment after.

What is powered land, and why does power drive the value?

Powered land is a site that already has a secured path to large amounts of electricity, either a grid connection with capacity reserved or energy that can be generated on the site. In a powered land data center deal, the buyer is mostly paying for that path. The acres matter less.

The reason is time. A new large load often has to wait in a utility's queue for studies and upgrades before it can connect. Our guide to the data center interconnection queue explains why those waits run long. Land that skips the queue lets a developer start sooner. That is what lifts its value.

There are two routes to powered land:

  • Grid-powered land. A utility has agreed to serve a large load at the site, or the land sits next to a substation with spare capacity.
  • Energy-powered land. The fuel or energy is on the site, and power is generated there, behind the meter. Pipeline gas, gathering-system gas, landfill gas and existing industrial sites with power plant infrastructure all belong here.

Some rural and energy-producing landowners are on the second route and don't know it. Our guide to gas-powered data centers covers how that works and why firm supply matters. For the scale of demand, see how much power a data center uses.

Should you sell, lease or partner?

Selling gives you money once and ends your involvement. A ground lease gives you rent over many years while you keep the title. A partnership adds a second income line, such as selling gas to the site. The trade-off is a longer, closer relationship.

Sell outright. You sell land for data center use and walk away. You give up any later rise in value. You usually can't undo it.

Ground lease. If you lease land for a data center, the developer builds and operates on it, often for decades, and pays you rent. You keep the land. At the end it returns to you or your heirs. Leases often include an option for the tenant to buy later, so read that clause closely.

Partnership on land and energy. This fits owners with more than land: gas production, a gathering line, a landfill or an industrial site with existing infrastructure. You might lease the land and sign a long-term gas supply agreement with the operator, so the site becomes both your tenant and your customer.

This guide is general information, not legal or tax advice. The right choice depends on your tax position, estate plans, title, existing leases and local law. Talk to a real estate lawyer and a tax advisor before you sign anything, including an option or a letter of intent.

How much do data centers lease land for?

There is no standard rate. What a data center pays to lease land depends mostly on how much usable power the site can deliver and how soon, then on fiber, zoning and how much work the site needs.

We don't publish land rates. Be careful with any number you read online. Headline sale prices usually come from sites that were already zoned, studied and promised power. Raw land without them is a different asset. What moves the value of yours:

  • how much firm power the site can support, and how quickly
  • whether zoning and permits are in place or still to win
  • how close fiber and roads are, and who pays to extend them
  • how much of the study and site work the developer must fund
  • whether you also bring energy, such as gas supply, to the deal
  • the length of the lease and whether rent rises over the term

The only reliable way to learn what your site is worth is a review of the site itself, ideally by more than one party.

How to sell land to a data center, step by step

Most deals follow the same order, sale or lease. Expect it to take a while.

  1. A first call. Share the location, rough size, what energy is on or near the land, and any known limits such as easements, mineral rights or existing leases.
  2. A site review under NDA. The developer looks at maps, title, utility or gas supply details and access. A non-disclosure agreement protects your information and theirs.
  3. Studies. Power and gas supply, environmental review, geotechnical work, a fiber route, water and permitting. Ask who pays for each study and who owns the results.
  4. An option or a letter of intent. An option gives the developer the right, not the duty, to buy or lease within a set period, usually for a fee. A letter of intent sets out the main terms before the full contract.
  5. Final agreements. A purchase agreement, or a ground lease, plus any energy supply, easement or access agreements.
  6. Closing or lease start. Money changes hands, or rent begins, and construction can follow once permits are in place.

Red flags and questions to ask before you sign

A low price hurts. Worse is land tied up for years in an option that never closes. Watch for these:

  • a long exclusive option with a small fee and no deadline to decide
  • pressure to sign before your lawyer has read the document
  • a buyer who won't say who they are, or who will build and operate the site
  • vague answers on how the site will get power or fuel
  • a promised price that depends on approvals you are expected to win alone
  • a lease with no clear plan for removing the buildings at the end

Questions worth asking any developer:

  • Who will own and operate the site, and have you built one before?
  • Where will the power come from, and is that supply firm?
  • Which studies do you need, who pays, and do I get copies?
  • How long is the option, what does it pay, and can it be extended?
  • What happens to my land, roads and water if the project stops?
  • In a lease, how does rent change over time, and who pays property tax?

Most sites don't fit, and that is normal

Most land is not a data center site, usually because of power, fiber or timing. A short review that ends in a clear no keeps you from tying the land up for nothing.

Common reasons a site doesn't fit: no power path within reach, gas supply that is interruptible or declining, no fiber close enough, floodplain or poor ground, or zoning the community is unlikely to change. Flare gas and interruptible gas suit uses that can switch off when supply drops, such as Bitcoin mining. Data centers need steady power.

Frequently asked questions

Can I lease my land to a data center instead of selling it?

Yes. Many developers prefer a long ground lease. So do many owners, since they keep the title and earn rent over the term. Read the option-to-buy, rent increase, property tax and end-of-lease removal clauses carefully, and have a real estate lawyer review the lease before you sign.

Do I need power on my land to sell it to a data center?

You need a credible path to power, not a finished connection. That can be spare capacity at a nearby substation that a utility is willing to commit, or energy on the land itself, such as pipeline gas, gathering-system gas or landfill gas that can generate power on site. Without either, most developers will pass, however good the rest of the land is.

How many acres does a data center need?

It depends more on how much power the site can deliver than on the land itself. A large campus with its own power plant and room to expand needs far more than a small site. Developers usually size a site from the power available, then check whether the acreage fits the building, the power equipment, setbacks and future phases.

Is powered land worth more than raw land?

Usually, yes, by a wide margin, because a secured power path saves a developer years of waiting and study cost. How much more depends on how much firm power is available, how soon, and what work remains. Headline sale prices don't transfer to raw land.

Is it better for taxes to sell or lease land to a data center?

It depends on your situation. This guide can't answer it for you. A sale and a lease are taxed differently, and the timing of income, your estate plans and any existing leases all matter. Ask a tax advisor to compare both before you sign an option or a letter of intent, since those documents can set the shape of the deal early.

I own gas wells or a gathering line. Can that power a data center?

It can, if the supply is firm: steady volumes, contracted, with backup supply. A data center runs around the clock, so it needs fuel it can count on for years. Gas that is interruptible, declining or currently flared is a poor match. It usually suits loads that can switch off. A short call and a review of your supply data will tell you which side you are on.
