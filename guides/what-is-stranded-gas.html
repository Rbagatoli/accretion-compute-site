What Is Stranded Gas? A Producer's Guide to the Options

Stranded is a market status, not a kind of gas. Here is what the term means, why gas gets stuck, and which uses fit gas that is steady, declining or on and off.

Updated October 28, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 7 min read

Quick answer

Stranded gas is natural gas that has been found or produced but cannot reach a buyer at a price that pays for getting it there. Usually there is no pipeline, the pipeline is full, or the volume is too small. It is often flared, vented or reinjected. Pipelines, trucked CNG or LNG, gas-to-liquids and on-site power can use it.

If you have asked what is stranded gas, the short version is that the gas is fine and the route to market is not. The same molecule can be stranded in one field and sold in the next county, or stranded this year and sold next year once a pipeline arrives. That makes stranded natural gas a question of economics more than geology.

This guide is written from the producer's side. It separates three terms that often get mixed up, uses public data to show how much gas goes unsold, and compares the options side by side, including on-site power for Bitcoin mining and for data centers. It is plain about which gas suits which use.

What does stranded gas mean?

The stranded gas meaning used in the industry is a natural gas resource that is known, and often already flowing, but has no economic way to reach a market. What these cases share is that moving the gas costs more than a buyer would pay for it.

There are two broad kinds:

  • Economically stranded. The gas can be produced, but there is no pipeline nearby, the pipeline is full, or the volume is too small to pay for a connection. This is the common case for producers.
  • Physically stranded. The gas sits too deep or under an obstacle. Better drilling moves some of it into the economic category over time.

Because stranded is a status, it can change. A new pipeline or a higher price can unstrand gas. A price drop, an outage or a full takeaway system can strand gas that was selling last month.

Stranded gas vs flare gas vs associated gas

Associated gas is about where the gas comes from. Stranded gas is about whether it can reach a market. Flared or vented gas is about what happens to it when it cannot.

  • Associated gas is produced along with crude oil. Alberta's regulator calls it solution gas. Oil is the reason the well was drilled, so the gas comes out whether or not there is a buyer.
  • Non-associated gas comes from wells drilled for gas. It can be stranded too, but it is less often flared, because nobody drills for gas without a plan to sell it.
  • Flared gas is burned at the site. Vented gas is released unburned. Both dispose of gas that cannot be sold, used or reinjected right now.

Not all flared gas is stranded: some flaring happens during well tests or maintenance. And not all stranded gas is flared: some is reinjected, shut in or never developed.

The volumes are large. The World Bank's Global Gas Flaring Tracker puts global flaring at 167 billion cubic meters in 2025, the third yearly rise in a row and the highest since 2019, worth an estimated US$54 billion. The US made the largest absolute cut of any country, down 0.4 bcm (7%). EIA data show US producers vented and flared about 335 billion cubic feet in 2024, less than 1 percent of the roughly 41.4 trillion cubic feet of marketed production that year.

In Alberta, operators flared 1,534.1 million cubic meters of gas in 2024, of which 914.5 million cubic meters was solution gas, up 19.3% on 2023, according to the Alberta Energy Regulator. The province still conserved 96.5% of its solution gas. The EPA notes that methane, the main part of natural gas, is more than 28 times as potent as carbon dioxide at trapping heat, so venting is worse than flaring, and using the gas beats both.

Why does natural gas get stranded?

Natural gas gets stranded when moving it costs more than it is worth at the end of the line. Gas is bulky compared with oil, so it needs a pipeline or expensive processing to travel, and that infrastructure only gets built where volumes and prices justify it. The common causes:

  • No pipeline. The well is far from a gathering system or transmission line, and the volume will not pay for a new lateral.
  • A full pipeline. In 2024, EIA reports, Permian Basin output outpaced pipeline takeaway, and gas at the Waha Hub in West Texas traded below zero on 42% of trading days. Producers were paying to have gas taken away until new capacity came online late in the year.
  • Low price or small volume. A few small or declining wells may not cover compression, gathering and processing, even with a pipeline nearby.
  • Gas quality or processing. Gas high in hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, nitrogen or liquids must be treated first, and a full processing plant leaves it nowhere to go.
  • Timing. An oil well can produce long before gathering lines reach it, so its associated gas is stranded while the system catches up.

The World Bank's 2026 tracker makes a similar point at global scale: the gas is already there, and what holds back its use is mainly the lack of pipelines, gas markets, capital and enforced regulation, not missing technology.

What can you do with stranded natural gas?

There are two ways to use stranded natural gas: move it to a market, or bring a market to it. The table after this section compares the main options on what each needs from the gas.

  • Pipeline or gathering tie-in. The best answer when volume and reserve life justify the cost. It turns stranded gas into ordinary sales gas.
  • CNG trucking. Compressed gas moves by tube trailer to a pipeline or customer. It works at small scale and can move between sites, but trucking cost limits distance.
  • Small-scale LNG. Liquefied gas packs more energy per truck, but the plant wants steady volume to pay back.
  • Gas-to-liquids (GTL). Gas becomes liquid fuels or chemicals such as methanol. Large plants need large, long-lived reserves.
  • Reinjection. Gas goes back into the reservoir, sometimes to support oil recovery. It defers the problem rather than earning revenue.
  • On-site power for field operations. Gas replaces diesel in generators and compressors on the lease. The load is small next to what a busy pad makes.
  • On-site power for Bitcoin mining. Generators at the well power mining machines. Mining can shut off in minutes and the equipment can move, so it copes with declining or on-and-off gas.
  • On-site power for an AI data center. A generation plant feeds AI hardware on a long-term lease. It can take a large, steady volume for many years, but only if supply is firm. See how a gas-powered data center works.

Flare gas or firm gas: which use fits yours?

The right use depends less on whether your gas is stranded and more on how steady it is and how long it will last. A declining oil well flaring its associated gas and a gas field with years of reserves behind a full pipeline are both stranded. They need different buyers.

Ask four questions about your gas:

  1. How long will it flow at a useful rate? Associated gas from new oil wells often falls quickly. A gas field with proven reserves can hold up much longer.
  2. How steady is it day to day? Gas that drops when the oil side is shut in is interruptible in practice, whatever the contract says.
  3. Is a pipeline coming? If a tie-in is likely soon, a use that can leave easily is worth more than one that needs a long commitment.
  4. Can the buyer's load stop without harm? This is the question that splits Bitcoin mining from AI computing.

Flare and interruptible gas suit Bitcoin mining better than an AI data center. A mining site can turn off when the gas dips, restart when it returns, and move when the wells decline.

An AI data center is the opposite. Tenants run hardware near full load around the clock on multi-year leases, so a fuel interruption is an outage. It needs gas that is steady, contracted for years and backed up, such as gathering-system gas with a pipeline tie behind it. Stranded gas meets that bar when takeaway limits or price strand it, not a short well life. That is the version that fits behind-the-meter power for data centers.

What should a producer check before selling gas to an on-site buyer?

Any on-site buyer, whether a miner, a GTL unit or a data center, will ask the same things first.

  1. Volume and profile. Daily rate, history and a forecast. For a long-term buyer, reserve life matters more than today's rate.
  2. Gas analysis. Composition, heating value, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, water and liquids. This sets the conditioning needed.
  3. Reliability. How often the gas stops today, and why.
  4. Backup supply. Whether a pipeline, a second gathering system or other wells can cover a shortfall. This decides whether the gas can be called firm.
  5. Land and access. Surface ownership, lease terms, road access and, for a data center, fiber within reach.
  6. Rules. Flaring limits, air permits for on-site generation, and any state or provincial approvals.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between stranded gas and flared gas?

Stranded gas is gas that cannot reach a market at a price that pays. Flared gas is gas burned at the site. They overlap, because much flaring happens when gas is stranded. But some stranded gas is reinjected or never produced, and some flaring is short-term, during well tests or maintenance. Stranded describes the market. Flared describes the disposal.

Is stranded gas the same as associated gas?

No. Associated gas is gas produced along with crude oil, named for where it comes from. Stranded gas is named for its market status. Associated gas is often stranded, because oil wells can come online before gathering lines catch up, but much of it is gathered and sold. Gas from wells drilled for gas can be stranded too.

Can stranded gas be used for Bitcoin mining?

Yes, and it is one of the better fits for flare and interruptible gas. Generators at the well site power mining machines in containers. Mining can stop within minutes when gas pressure drops, restart when it returns, and move when wells decline or a pipeline arrives. The trade-offs are exposure to Bitcoin prices and running generators in the field.

Can stranded gas power an AI data center?

Only if the gas is firm. AI tenants run hardware near full load around the clock on multi-year leases, so the fuel has to be steady, contracted for years and backed up. Gas stranded by takeaway limits or low prices, with long reserve life and a second supply path, can meet that bar. Gas from declining wells or flare gas cannot, and is better suited to a load that can switch off.
