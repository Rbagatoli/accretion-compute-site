Data Center Cooling Systems: Every Method, by Rack Density

How heat leaves a server and then leaves the building. Each method, the rack density it suits, what it means for water, and what a tenant has to specify.

Updated October 29, 2026 · By Accretion Compute team · 6 min read

Quick answer

Data center cooling systems do two jobs. They capture heat at the IT equipment, using room air (CRAC or CRAH units), aisle containment, rear-door coils, direct-to-chip cold plates or immersion. Then they reject it outdoors through chillers, cooling towers, dry coolers or free cooling. Conventional racks run on air; AI racks near 100 kW need liquid.

Data center cooling systems are usually described as a list of products. It is easier to see them as a chain: heat moves from the chips into air or liquid, then to a heat exchanger, then out of the site through a chiller, cooling tower or dry cooler.

This guide walks that chain and sorts the main data center cooling technologies by the rack density they suit. Figures come from the Department of Energy (DOE) and its labs, ASHRAE's guidelines as summarized by DOE, and Uptime Institute's survey. We left out the kW "limits" quoted online that we could not trace to a source.

How does data center cooling work?

Data center cooling moves heat from the IT equipment to the outdoors in two stages. PNNL notes that most of the electricity IT equipment uses is turned into heat, so the cooling system has to carry away close to what the racks draw.

  1. Capture. Heat leaves the chips and enters a medium: room air, air passing through a coil at the rack, liquid in a cold plate, or a fluid the server sits in.
  2. Transport. Air handlers or coolant distribution units (CDUs) pass that heat into a facility water loop.
  3. Rejection. A chiller, cooling tower, dry cooler or outside air takes the heat out of the loop and releases it to the atmosphere.

The target at the server inlet comes from ASHRAE. Its recommended range for air-cooled IT equipment is 18 to 27 °C (64.4 to 80.6 °F), and DOE's design guide says to design and run to it.

Cooling is the largest overhead. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) says cooling dominates facility infrastructure energy, and infrastructure fell from 36% of US data center electricity in 2018 to 31% in 2024, helped by more AI servers in liquid-cooled facilities.

Air cooling: CRAC, CRAH and aisle containment

Room air cooling is still the default for conventional racks. Cold air reaches the front of the racks, server fans pull it through, and warm air leaves the back.

Two kinds of unit do the work:

  • CRAC (computer room air conditioner). A packaged unit with its own compressor and refrigerant. DOE calls packaged direct-expansion units the most common cooling equipment in smaller data centers. Heat is rejected through an air-cooled condenser, a dry cooler or a cooling tower.
  • CRAH (computer room air handler). Fans and a coil fed with chilled water from a central plant. There is no compressor in the unit itself. Large sites usually pair CRAHs, often in rooms next to the hall, with a central chilled water plant.

Central systems tend to waste less. DOE's guide compares two large facilities with similar IT loads. The one with a central air handler system put almost two-thirds of its input power into IT equipment. The one with many distributed CRAC units put in just over one-third.

Containment helps either kind. Racks face each other in alternating cold and hot aisles, and gaps are sealed so the air streams don't mix. With separation, return air often comes back at 85 °F or more, which DOE notes extends the hours a site can use free cooling.

Close-coupled cooling: in-row coolers and rear-door heat exchangers

Close-coupled cooling moves the air-to-liquid heat exchange from the edge of the room to the rack itself. DOE groups these as localized air-to-liquid heat exchangers.

  • Rear-door heat exchangers (cooling doors) replace a rack's back door with a water coil. Passive doors rely on server fans; active doors add their own.
  • In-row coolers sit between racks and pull air from the hot aisle.

The servers stay air-cooled, so this suits hardware with no liquid connections. It is often how a hall handles racks denser than its room units were designed for.

Liquid cooling: direct-to-chip and immersion

Direct liquid cooling takes heat into a fluid at the chip, before it ever reaches room air. DOE says liquid can serve higher heat densities and be much more efficient than air, because water carries heat far better.

  • Direct-to-chip (cold plates). The heat sinks on processors are replaced by plates with liquid channels, fed through a CDU. Most of the heat goes to liquid. The rest, from parts without plates, still goes to air, so these halls are hybrids.
  • Single-phase immersion. Servers sit in a tank of nonconductive fluid that is pumped through a heat exchanger.
  • Two-phase immersion. An engineered fluid boils at the hot components, and the vapor condenses on a coil and drips back.

High-performance computing adopted liquid early: DOE's guide notes racks of 60 kW in 2013 and recent ones above 125 kW. Warmer coolant also allows more free cooling and, in some cases, dry coolers in place of towers.

Heat rejection: chillers, cooling towers, dry coolers and free cooling

Heat rejection is the outdoor half of the system, and it decides most of a site's water use.

Data center cooling towers reject heat by evaporation. DOE gives the water balance as make-up water equals evaporation plus blowdown plus drift. Blowdown is water drained to stop minerals building up, and it is the part operators control most.

Free cooling uses cold outside air or water instead of compressors. DOE says a water-side economizer suits climates with wet-bulb temperatures below 55 °F for 3,000 or more hours a year. Its list of steps for sustainable sites puts free cooling first and rejecting remaining heat to dry coolers third.

Which cooling system fits which rack density?

Rack density is the main thing that decides the capture method. The sourced anchors are:

  • A few kW. LBNL says a rack of conventional servers used about 3 to 5 kW. Room air with CRAC or CRAH units handles this.
  • About 10 to 30 kW. Uptime Institute's 2025 survey says average densities are rising slowly, driven by more racks in the 10 to 30 kW range, and that few facilities exceed 30 kW. Containment, and then rear doors or in-row coolers, carry air cooling into this range.
  • 100 kW and up. LBNL says AI racks use as much as 100 kW. A December 2025 Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) report for DOE puts AI training racks above 100 kW and present-day GPU racks at 120 kW. These are liquid-cooled, usually with cold plates.

There is no single number where air stops working. DOE's guide points to an ASHRAE white paper that ties the move to liquid to the power of each chip, not rack totals. In practice the hardware spec settles it: a server built with cold plates needs liquid at the rack.

Most dense halls end up mixed: cold plates for accelerators, air for the rest. To size the heat behind each density, see how much power a data center uses.

What should a tenant specify for cooling?

If you bring your own hardware, the cooling questions start with your servers. Settle these in writing before a hall is built:

  1. Rack power, now and later. Day-one kW per rack and the density expected over the lease.
  2. Air, liquid or both. For cold-plate servers, the share of heat that goes to liquid.
  3. Coolant temperature class. ASHRAE names liquid classes by upper supply temperature in °C: W17, W27, W32, W40, W45 and W+. Warmer classes allow more free cooling.
  4. Flow, pressure and fluid your hardware maker allows.
  5. CDU and manifold ownership. Who supplies and maintains them.
  6. Resilience. Spare cooling units, and whether pumps and fans ride on UPS. PNNL notes this is increasingly common in AI sites, because dense racks heat up fast.
  7. Water limits. Whether the site can run towers or must reject heat dry.

That split is the core of GPU colocation: you own the servers, and the provider delivers power, cooling and space to your specification.

Frequently asked questions

How are data centers cooled?

In two stages. Inside, heat is captured by room air from CRAC or CRAH units, by coils on the racks, or by liquid in cold plates or immersion tanks. Outside, it is rejected by chillers, cooling towers, dry coolers or outside air. Conventional racks use air; dense AI racks use liquid at the chip.

What is the difference between CRAC and CRAH units?

A CRAC (computer room air conditioner) has its own compressor and refrigerant, and is common in smaller data centers. A CRAH (computer room air handler) has no compressor: it blows air over a coil fed with chilled water from a central plant. Large sites usually use CRAHs with a central plant.

What temperature should a data center be kept at?

ASHRAE recommends a server inlet air temperature of 18 to 27 °C (64.4 to 80.6 °F) for air-cooled equipment. That is the air entering the servers, not the room average. The wider allowable ranges show what hardware tolerates, not what keeps it reliable.

Do data centers use cooling towers?

Many large data centers do, usually to reject heat from a chilled water plant or to run a water-side economizer. A tower cools water by evaporating part of it, so it uses water continuously. DOE's guidance puts make-up water as evaporation plus blowdown plus drift, and says raising cycles of concentration from three to six cuts make-up water by 20% and blowdown by 50%. Sites short of water can use dry coolers instead.

What is free cooling in a data center?

Free cooling uses cold outside air or water instead of compressors. An air-side economizer brings filtered outside air into the hall. A water-side economizer uses a cooling tower to chill the facility loop. DOE says many cool-climate data centers run with no chillers at all.

At what rack density do you need liquid cooling?

There is no single cutoff; it depends on how much power each chip draws and on the hall design. As anchors, conventional racks use about 3 to 5 kW and run on air, while AI racks reach 100 kW or more and are liquid-cooled. Containment and rear doors extend air in between.
